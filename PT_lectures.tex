\noindent\textbf{Conventions.} Conformal time $\tau$, defined by $\dd t = a\,\dd\tau$, so that $\tau = \int\dd t/a$ --- an integral, not $t/a$; comoving coordinates $\vx$; $\cH \equiv \dd\ln a/\dd\tau = aH$; overdots denote $\partial/\partial\tau$. The comoving background 3-metric is flat, and spatial indices are raised and lowered with $\delta_{ij}$ --- so a repeated pair is summed regardless of placement, and $\partial_k\Phi\,\partial_k\Phi$ means $\sum_k(\partial_k\Phi)^2$. (Under the general-relativistic convention one would move them with $g_{ij}$ instead, which would fold the factor $a^2(1-2\phi)$ into every contraction.) Fourier transforms follow
\begin{equation*}
\delta(\vk) = \int \dd^3x\, e^{-i\vk\cdot\vx}\,\delta(\vx),
\qquad
\delta(\vx) = \int_{\vk} e^{i\vk\cdot\vx}\,\delta(\vk),
\qquad
\int_{\vk} \equiv \int\!\frac{\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3},
\end{equation*}
so that $\langle\delta(\vk)\delta(\vk')\rangle = (2\pi)^3\dirac(\vk+\vk')P(k)$. A primed correlator, $\langle\cdots\rangle'$, strips the factor $(2\pi)^3\dirac$. This is the convention of both Dodelson \& Schmidt \cite[Eqs.~(5.26)--(5.27)]{DodelsonSchmidt} and Jeong \cite[App.~A.1]{Jeong2010} --- the two sources these lectures lean on hardest --- so formulae can be carried across from either without adjustment. Not every reference agrees: some place the $(2\pi)^{-3}$ on $\dd^3x$ instead, and their power spectra then differ by factors of $(2\pi)^3$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{The setting.} Fixed for the whole course: a spatially flat
Friedmann--Lema\^itre--Robertson--Walker (FLRW) universe --- homogeneous,
isotropic and expanding --- with everything of interest a small departure from
it. The unperturbed metric is
\begin{equation*}
\dd s^2 = a^2(\tau)\left[-\dd\tau^2 + \delta_{ij}\,\dd x^i\dd x^j\right],
\end{equation*}
written in comoving coordinates $\vx$, which ride along with the expansion: a
galaxy at rest keeps the same $\vx$ while its physical separation from us,
$a\vx$, grows. \begin{reading}
Conformal time is used throughout because of the form this takes:
with $\dd\tau$ and $\dd\vx$ appearing symmetrically, radial light rays obey
$\dd x = \pm\dd\tau$, so light travels at unit comoving speed and an interval of
conformal time \emph{is} a comoving distance. That is what will make the horizon
a clean notion in Section~\ref{sec:bridge}.
\end{reading}

Structure is what happens when this is not quite true. The metric
picks up small perturbations, and the matter density departs from its mean by
\begin{equation*}
\delta(\vx,\tau) \equiv \frac{\rho(\vx,\tau) - \bar\rho(\tau)}{\bar\rho(\tau)} ,
\end{equation*}
with $|\delta|\ll1$ early on and of order unity by the time there are galaxies to
count. How $\delta$ gets from one to the other is the whole course.

\medskip
\begin{reading}
\noindent\textbf{Two ways to hold the same field.} We move between them
constantly, so it is worth saying once that they carry identical information.
In \emph{position space}, $\delta(\vx)$ is a lumpy field filling the universe;
this is where physical intuition lives, and where the displacements and
collapsing sheets of Lecture~2 are easiest to picture. In
\emph{Fourier space}, $\delta(\vk)$ is the amplitude of one plane wave of
comoving wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/k$ --- so large $k$ means small scales, and
$k = 0.1\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ means $\lambda \simeq 63\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}$, a few
cluster diameters. This is where the dynamics and the statistics live. The
transform convention is in the front matter.

The linear problem wants the second picture, and not merely for convenience: the
linearized equations have coefficients that do not depend on position, because
the background is homogeneous, so a plane wave stays a plane wave and each
$\delta(\vk)$ evolves independently of every other. One partial differential
equation in three dimensions becomes one ordinary differential equation per
$\vk$ --- which is what makes linear theory solvable at all.
Figure~\ref{fig:fourier} is the same statement drawn.
\end{reading}

\subsection{The equations we will use, and why we may}
\label{sec:bridge}

From here on everything is Newtonian: a fluid in an expanding box, no metric in
sight. Three equations do all the work. This section says what they are, what was
discarded to reach them, and --- more useful than either --- what discarding it
leaves us free to do.

\paragraph{The metric, and two potentials.} Fix the gauge completely by writing
the perturbed metric in conformal Newtonian (longitudinal) form
\cite{MaBertschinger1995},
\begin{equation}
\dd s^2 = a^2(\tau)\left[-(1+2\psi)\,\dd\tau^2
+ (1-2\phi)\,\delta_{ij}\,\dd x^i\dd x^j\right] .
\label{eq:newtonian_gauge}
\end{equation}
Here $\psi$ perturbs $g_{00}$ --- it is what a slow particle falls down --- and
$\phi$ perturbs the spatial metric --- it is what appears in the field equation.
\begin{reading}
Newtonian gravity has one potential, not two, so we need these to coincide. They
do whenever the total anisotropic stress vanishes
\cite[Eq.~(23d)]{MaBertschinger1995}, which holds for cold dark matter and
baryons in the matter era; free-streaming neutrinos spoil it at the $15\%$ level
during radiation domination, but that contribution fades as matter comes to
dominate. Write $\phi = \psi \equiv \Phi$.
\end{reading}

\begin{quiz}
Gravity is weak everywhere in the universe --- the potential is of order
$10^{-5}$ on every scale. So why is ``gravity is weak'' not enough to justify
using Newtonian dynamics?
\nudge{Split it. Is a super-horizon mode weakly gravitating? Yes. Can you write
down a Newtonian description of one? Then weakness cannot be what licenses it.}
\answer{Two different approximations are being conflated, and weakness is only
one of them. A weak field, $|\Phi| \sim 10^{-5}$, licenses linearizing the
metric --- and that holds on every scale. A Newtonian description needs
something more: the Poisson equation acts instantaneously, and instantaneous
action only makes sense if light can cross the scale of interest in much less
time than the system takes to evolve, which is one expansion time. That is the
sub-horizon condition $k \gg \cH$. A super-horizon mode fails it no matter how
weak gravity is: no signal has crossed the mode even once, so there is no
settled potential for Poisson to describe. So: weak field on all scales,
Newtonian dynamics only inside the horizon.}
\end{quiz}
\paragraph{The equations.} Now take the second limit --- the one the question
above was pointing at --- and keep only scales far inside the horizon,
$k \gg \cH$. The system is three physical statements, each worth deriving in
words before writing it down.

\emph{Counting matter.} The contrast changes only because matter flows. Mass
conservation for the peculiar velocity $\vu \equiv \dd\vx/\dd\tau$ reads
$\dot\delta = -\nabla\cdot[(1+\delta)\vu]$ --- dilution by the expansion is
already inside $\bar\rho$, so it never appears in $\delta$ --- and at linear
order $\dot\delta = -\theta$, with $\theta \equiv \nabla\cdot\vu$.

\emph{Pushing matter.} For a pressureless fluid there is no separate Euler
equation to import: Euler \emph{is} Newton's second law applied to fluid
elements. In comoving coordinates it reads
\begin{equation}
\ddot\vx + \cH\,\dot\vx = -\nabla\Phi .
\label{eq:newtonian_eom}
\end{equation}
The new term is the drag $\cH\dot\vx$, and it is not friction: set $\Phi = 0$
and \eqref{eq:newtonian_eom} says $\dot\vx \propto 1/a$ --- free particles coast
to rest in comoving coordinates as their peculiar velocities redshift away.
(Exercise~\ref{ex:geodesic} derives it from the geodesic equation of
\eqref{eq:newtonian_gauge}.) Lecture~2 is built on this equation --- it follows
elements rather than fields, and that change of variable is the whole of the
Lagrangian picture. For now, take its divergence:
$\dot\theta = -\cH\,\theta - \nabla^2\Phi$.

\emph{Sourcing gravity.} What closes the system is the field equation, and here
the sub-horizon limit earns its keep: every term carrying a factor of $\cH$
where its neighbours carry $k$ is suppressed by $(\cH/k)^2$, and the energy
constraint of general relativity collapses to the Poisson equation,
$\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi Ga^2\bar\rho\,\delta = \tfrac32\Om(\tau)\cH^2\delta$, the
last equality by the Friedmann equation. (The same suppression removes a
dilation term $3\dot\phi$ from continuity above.) Exercise~\ref{ex:subhorizon}
carries out the reduction and puts a number on what was dropped.

Assembled, at linear order:
\begin{equation}
\dot\delta = -\theta,
\qquad
\dot\theta = -\cH\,\theta - \nabla^2\Phi,
\qquad
\nabla^2\Phi = \frac{3}{2}\Om(\tau)\cH^2\delta .
\label{eq:newtonian_linear}
\end{equation}
Ma \& Bertschinger \cite[\S 5]{MaBertschinger1995} give the unapproximated
system; Dodelson \& Schmidt \cite[Ch.~12]{DodelsonSchmidt} set out this same
starting point with its assumptions listed.

\begin{remark}
Which assumption lives where. The three members of \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear}
do not lean on the same legs, and it is worth being precise.
\emph{Continuity} is the most robust: mass conservation, needing only
$|\delta| \ll 1$ to linearize and one use of $k \gg \cH$, to drop the
relativistic dilation term $3\dot\phi$. \emph{Euler}, in the form
$\dot\theta = -\cH\theta - \nabla^2\Phi$, needs the matter to be cold ---
pressureless and single-stream --- plus $u \ll 1$ and $|\Phi| \ll 1$, but
\emph{no} sub-horizon limit at all: for cold matter it is exact at linear order
in this gauge. \emph{Poisson} is where $k \gg \cH$ does its real work: the
relativistic energy constraint carries extra terms of relative size
$(\cH/k)^2$, and dropping them is what turns a constraint of general relativity
into Newton's field equation --- Exercise~\ref{ex:subhorizon} makes this
quantitative. Finally, one assumption underwrites the system as a whole rather
than any single member: $\phi = \psi$, because Euler feels the time--time
potential while Poisson sources the spatial one.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{What \texorpdfstring{$(\cH/k)^2$}{(H/k)2} buys.} One small parameter
did the work above, and it does a further job that the whole of Lecture~2 rests
on.

\emph{It keeps the metric linear.} Inverting the Poisson equation,
\begin{equation}
|\Phi| \sim \frac{3}{2}\,\Om\left(\frac{\cH}{k}\right)^{\!2}\delta ,
\label{eq:phi_small}
\end{equation}
the potential is the density contrast suppressed by two powers of $\cH/k$. At
$k = 0.1\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ that factor is about $10^{-5}$, so even a patch with
$\delta \sim 100$ carries $|\Phi| \sim 5\times10^{-4}$. Gravity stays weak
precisely where clustering becomes strong --- which is the licence for everything
that follows. We will let $\delta$ grow past unity and resum it to all orders
through displacements, while never going beyond linear order in the metric. Those
two statements sound contradictory and are not: $\Phi$ and $\delta$ are separated
by $(\cH/k)^2$, and it is $\delta$ alone that becomes large.

\begin{reading}
\emph{It also makes $\delta$ meaningful.} Relativistically $\delta$ is not a physical
quantity: it depends on how spacetime is sliced. The same mode grows as
$(k\tau)^2$ in synchronous gauge and sits frozen in the Newtonian gauge until
horizon entry \cite[\S 7]{MaBertschinger1995} --- which is why ``do perturbations
grow outside the horizon?'' has no gauge-independent answer. Sub-horizon that
ambiguity is suppressed by the same $(\cH/k)^2$, so in the regime of
\eqref{eq:newtonian_linear}, and only there, $\delta$ becomes something
unambiguous to compute, correlate and measure. $\Phi$ carries no such
restriction: \eqref{eq:newtonian_gauge} fixes the gauge completely, leaving no
residual freedom, so $\Phi$ is physical on \emph{every} scale --- it is the
Bardeen potential under another name. That is exactly why
Section~\ref{sec:linearpk} follows $\Phi$ and not $\delta$ when it has to cross
the horizon.
\end{reading}


\begin{remark}
Where relativity actually enters. Everything these lectures \emph{compute} is
Newtonian and sub-horizon: the system \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear}, the linear
growth it implies, and the whole of Lecture~2. Beyond the FLRW background
itself, general relativity appears exactly three times, each as an input rather
than a calculation: the metric \eqref{eq:newtonian_gauge} with $\phi = \psi$,
which defines $\Phi$; the primordial spectrum of Section~\ref{sec:primordial},
which is inflation's output; and the one super-horizon fact that $\Phi$ holds
still outside the horizon (Section~\ref{sec:linearpk}), used only to carry the
initial condition intact to horizon entry. One dynamical ingredient is quoted
rather than derived: the acoustic oscillation of the radiation fluid, which
needs pressure and so lies outside \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear}. Everything else
is the three equations above.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The pressureless fluid, and how perturbations grow}
\label{sec:vlasov}

\begin{reading}
We now set up the nonlinear problem in the Newtonian regime, following \cite[\S 2]{Bernardeau2002}. Particles obey \eqref{eq:newtonian_eom}, with $\Phi$ the peculiar potential of \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear}. Collisionless matter is then described by its phase-space density $f(\vx,\vp,\tau)$, with $\vp = am\vu$; phase-space conservation gives the Vlasov equation,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\tau} + \frac{\vp}{ma}\cdot\nabla f - am\,\nabla\Phi\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vp} = 0 .
\label{eq:vlasov}
\end{equation}
Taking velocity moments produces a hierarchy: the zeroth moment gives continuity, the first gives the Euler equation, and each couples to the next through the velocity-dispersion tensor $\sigma_{ij} \equiv \langle u_iu_j\rangle_p - \langle u_i\rangle_p\langle u_j\rangle_p$, where $\langle\cdot\rangle_p$ denotes the momentum average over $f$ at fixed $(\vx,\tau)$ (not the ensemble average of Section~\ref{sec:stats}):
\begin{align}
\dot\delta + \nabla\cdot\left[(1+\delta)\,\vu\right] &= 0 ,
\label{eq:continuity}\\
\dot{\vu} + \cH\,\vu + (\vu\cdot\nabla)\,\vu &= -\nabla\Phi - \frac{1}{\rho}\,\nabla_j\!\left(\rho\,\sigma_{ij}\right).
\label{eq:euler}
\end{align}
Cold matter starts cold: before orbits cross, each position hosts a single stream and $\sigma_{ij} = 0$. This \emph{single-stream closure} truncates the hierarchy into a pressureless perfect fluid. It is a closure rather than a controlled approximation: exact until shell crossing, and completely wrong after. Where it fails is where Lecture~2 stops.
\end{reading}

Everything needed is already in \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear}. One check first:
the curl of \eqref{eq:newtonian_eom} at linear order gives
$\dot{\bm{w}} + \cH\,\bm{w} = 0$ for the vorticity $\bm{w} = \nabla\times\vu$,
so rotation decays as $1/a$ and stays zero if it starts zero --- the velocity
field is fully specified by its divergence $\theta$. Now combine all three:
differentiate $\dot\delta = -\theta$, substitute $\dot\theta$, and eliminate
$\nabla^2\Phi$ with Poisson. No coefficient in the result carries a $k$, so the
position dependence factors out and every mode grows by one common factor,
\begin{equation}
\ddot D + \cH\,\dot D - \frac{3}{2}\Om(\tau)\,\cH^2 D = 0 .
\label{eq:growth}
\end{equation}
The growing solution $D_+(\tau)$ defines linear growth, $\delta^{(1)}(\vx,\tau) = D_+(\tau)\,\delta_0(\vx)$; in Einstein--de Sitter (EdS, $\Om = 1$) $D_+ = a$ and the decaying mode is $a^{-3/2}$. The velocity follows from continuity, and introduces the growth rate $f$ (not to be confused with the phase-space density, the one unfortunate collision in the standard notation),
\begin{equation}
\theta^{(1)} = -\cH f\,\delta^{(1)},
\qquad
f \equiv \frac{\dd\ln D_+}{\dd\ln a}
\simeq \Om^{5/9}(\tau) \quad\text{(flat \lcdm)} ,
\label{eq:growth_rate}
\end{equation}
with $f = 1$ in EdS. The often-quoted exponent $0.55$~\cite{Linder2005} agrees
with $5/9$ at the sub-percent level over viable cosmologies.

\paragraph{Solving it.} Einstein--de Sitter is the case to do by hand, because
everything else is a numerical perturbation on it. There $\cH = 2/\tau$ and
$a \propto \tau^2$, so \eqref{eq:growth} becomes
$\ddot D + (2/\tau)\dot D - (6/\tau^2)D = 0$. Try $D \propto \tau^n$ and the
indicial equation factorizes as $(n-2)(n+3) = 0$, giving $D \propto \tau^{2} = a$ and $D \propto \tau^{-3} = a^{-3/2}$. Two modes,
one growing and one decaying, as a second-order equation must have. The decaying
one is why initial conditions are forgettable: any admixture dies as $a^{-3/2}$,
so a universe started slightly wrong converges on the growing solution rather
than remembering the error. Every later statement in this course assumes only the
growing mode is left.

The growing mode is remarkable for how \emph{slow} it is. In a static medium an
overdensity grows exponentially; expansion converts that runaway into a power
law. The Friedmann equation makes $\cH^2 \sim Ga^2\bar\rho$, so the drag
$\cH\dot D$ and the source $\tfrac32\Om\cH^2 D$ in \eqref{eq:growth} are always
comparable: gravity wins, but only just. That is why
perturbations of order $10^{-3}$ at recombination are of order unity now, after a
factor of a thousand in $a$, instead of having collapsed long ago --- and why
perturbation theory is useful across five decades of expansion.

\begin{reading}
\paragraph{And in our universe.} Once dark energy takes over, the expansion
accelerates, the drag wins, and growth stalls. Solving \eqref{eq:growth}
numerically for flat \lcdm{} with $\Om = 0.31$ gives a present-day growth factor
about $22\%$ below what the matter-era $D_+ \propto a$ would have delivered, and
the growth rate falls from $f = 1$ deep in the matter era to $f(z{=}0) = 0.52$, matching $\Om^{5/9} = 0.522$ to the digits shown. Structure formation is, in this
sense, already over: the universe is coasting, and what we observe is close to
the final state of linear growth.
\end{reading}

\subsection{Gaussian random fields and the power spectrum}
\label{sec:stats}

Inflation delivers, to very good approximation, Gaussian initial conditions: the linear field $\delta^{(1)}(\vk)$ is a Gaussian random field, meaning that any finite set of its Fourier modes is jointly Gaussian with zero mean. Such a field is characterized completely by its power spectrum,
\begin{equation}
\langle \delta^{(1)}(\vk)\,\delta^{(1)}(\vk')\rangle = (2\pi)^3\dirac(\vk+\vk')\,\Plin(k) ,
\label{eq:plin_def}
\end{equation}
where two distinct assumptions are at work. Statistical \emph{homogeneity} --- invariance under translations --- is what forces the delta function, so that modes whose wavevectors do not sum to zero are uncorrelated. Statistical \emph{isotropy} is a separate statement, invariance under rotations, and it is what makes $\Plin$ a function of the magnitude $k=|\vk|$ alone rather than of the vector $\vk$.

\begin{reading}
Note the one pair that is exempt: $\vk' = -\vk$, where the delta function fires. Since the field is real, $\delta(-\vk) = \delta(\vk)^*$, so that case is $\langle|\delta(\vk)|^2\rangle$ --- a variance, and necessarily non-negative. It is not a counterexample to ``uncorrelated'' but the definition of $\Plin$ itself.

\paragraph{Making one.} That a Gaussian field is fixed completely by $\Plin$ is a
practical statement as much as a formal one, and it is what the hands-on session
begins with. On a periodic grid the recipe is three lines:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item for each mode $\vk$, draw a complex amplitude with zero mean and variance
$\Plin(k)$ --- equivalently, draw the modulus from a Rayleigh distribution and
the phase uniformly on $[0,2\pi)$;
\item impose reality, $\delta(-\vk) = \delta(\vk)^*$, so that the field you get
back is real rather than complex;
\item inverse transform.
\end{enumerate}
Nothing else is needed. Two realizations drawn with different random seeds are
different universes with identical statistics, which is why a fixed seed is the
right way to compare cosmologies: change $n_s$ with the seed held, and every
difference you see is physics rather than luck.

\paragraph{One number to fix the amplitude.} The spectrum has a shape and an
overall size, and the size is conventionally quoted as $\sigma_8$: the rms
fluctuation of the linear density field smoothed on spheres of radius
$8\,h^{-1}$Mpc,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_8^2 = \int\!\frac{\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\;\Plin(k)\,\big|W(kR)\big|^2 ,
\qquad R = 8\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc},
\label{eq:sigma8}
\end{equation}
with $W$ the Fourier transform of a top-hat sphere. The radius is historical
rather than fundamental --- it is roughly where $\sigma \simeq 1$, so it marks
the scale that is going nonlinear now --- but the definition is what normalizes
every spectrum you will plot. Measured, $\sigma_8 \simeq 0.81$.
\end{reading}

\begin{quiz}
Two realizations of the same $\Plin(k)$ with different seeds look completely
different. In what sense, then, is $\Plin$ a prediction of the theory at all?
\nudge{Which of these could you measure from one universe: the amplitude of the
mode at one particular $\vk$, or the average of $|\delta(\vk)|^2$ over all modes
of the same $|\vk|$? Which had better be the thing theory predicts?}
\answer{The theory predicts the distribution, not the draw. The phases ---
everything that makes two seeds look different --- are random and carry no
cosmology. $\Plin$ is the \emph{variance} of the modes, and the variance is the
prediction. We measure it from our single universe by averaging
$|\delta(\vk)|^2$ over the many modes that share the same $|\vk|$, which works
because they are independent draws from one distribution. The largest scales
have the fewest modes to average over, so they are the worst measured ---
cosmic variance is the price of owning one universe.}
\end{quiz}

\subsection{The spectrum inflation hands us}
\label{sec:primordial}

Everything so far has been about how a perturbation \emph{evolves}. Where the
perturbations came from is the business of the lectures you have just had, and
we need only their output: a nearly \emph{scale-invariant} spectrum of curvature
perturbations,
\begin{equation}
\Delta^2_\zeta(k) = A_s\left(\frac{k}{k_0}\right)^{n_s-1} ,
\qquad n_s \simeq 0.965 ,
\label{eq:primordial}
\end{equation}
with $A_s$ an amplitude and $k_0$ an arbitrary pivot scale.

\begin{reading}
Scale invariance means equal power in every logarithmic interval of $k$, which is
what $n_s = 1$ would give. Inflation cannot deliver it exactly: inflation has to
end, so the expansion rate drifts while modes are leaving the horizon, and the
spectrum tilts. The measured few-percent deficit is a genuine prediction rather
than a fitted convenience, and it is one of the reasons to believe the picture.
\end{reading}

Two consequences carry forward. The primordial field is Gaussian to excellent
approximation, which is what Section~\ref{sec:stats} assumed. And converting
curvature to matter density costs two powers of $k$ in the field, by the Poisson
equation, hence four in the spectrum, so the primordial \emph{matter} spectrum is
a featureless power law, $P_{\rm prim}(k) \propto k^{n_s}$. Nothing in that has a scale in it. Every feature in the observed $\Plin(k)$ ---
the turnover, the falloff, the wiggles --- is put there afterwards.

\subsection{Horizon crossing, and the shape of \texorpdfstring{$\Plin(k)$}{P(k)}}
\label{sec:linearpk}

Write the linear spectrum as the primordial one times a \emph{transfer function}
that carries all the processing:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\Plin(k,z) \;=\; A\,k^{n_s}\,T^2(k)\,D_+^2(z)\;}
\label{eq:pk_master}
\end{equation}
with $T \to 1$ on the largest scales by convention and $A$ fixed by one measured
amplitude, for which we use $\sigma_8$. The three factors are three separate
pieces of physics: inflation sets $k^{n_s}$, the growth factor $D_+^2$ carries
the time dependence of Section~\ref{sec:vlasov}, and $T^2(k)$ carries everything
in between. This is the formula you will code in the hands-on session, and the
question now is what shapes $T$.

\begin{quiz}
The primordial spectrum is a featureless power law. The observed one has a
turnover, a falloff and wiggles. Where does a \emph{scale} come from, when the
initial conditions contain none?
\nudge{No scale is primordial, so every one of them entered afterwards. One is
set by the expansion: when did a given mode come into causal contact? Another is
set by the contents: when did matter take over from radiation? A third is a
distance a wave could travel. Match each to a feature.}
\answer{Every scale in the observed spectrum was imprinted after inflation, by
two different pieces of physics. The turnover and the $k^{-2}$ falloff come from
horizon entry: modes that entered during radiation domination are suppressed,
and the boundary is $k_{\rm eq}$, the mode entering exactly at matter--radiation
equality. The wiggles come from a different scale entirely: the sound horizon at
recombination, $r_d \simeq 100\,h^{-1}$Mpc, the farthest a pressure wave in the
photon--baryon plasma travelled before the photons were released. Inflation
supplies only the amplitude and the tilt; every feature is processing.}
\end{quiz}

One thing does: \textbf{when a mode entered the horizon}.

\paragraph{The horizon.} Recall $\cH \equiv \dd\ln a/\dd\tau = aH$, the expansion
rate in conformal time. Because $\cH$ is a logarithmic rate, $1/\cH$ is a
\emph{time} --- roughly one e-folding of expansion; and because light travels at
unit comoving speed in conformal time, that same number is a \emph{length}: the
comoving distance light covers while the universe expands appreciably. This is the comoving Hubble radius, loosely
``the horizon''. A mode is \emph{sub-horizon} when $k \gg \cH$, so that a signal
crosses it many times per expansion time and gravity acts coherently across it,
and \emph{super-horizon} when $k \ll \cH$, where nothing propagates across it at
all.

\begin{reading}
Not the Hubble parameter $H = \dd\ln a/\dd t$: overdots here are
$\partial/\partial\tau$ throughout, so $\dot a/a$ is $\cH$, not $H$, and it is
also why the growth equation \eqref{eq:growth} carries $\cH\dot D$ where its
cosmic-time form carries $2H\,\dd D/\dd t$.

You met this quantity in the previous lectures, running the other way. During
inflation $1/\cH$ \emph{shrinks}, which is how modes exit and how their
fluctuations are frozen in as the initial conditions of
Section~\ref{sec:primordial}. Afterwards it grows, through radiation and matter
domination alike, so a mode --- labelled by a comoving $\vk$ that never changes
--- starts super-horizon and \emph{enters} when $k = \cH$. Nothing about the mode
changes at that moment; the horizon moves past it.
\end{reading}

What follows is what that
crossing does, and Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon} is the picture to keep in mind while
it happens.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{horizon_crossing.pdf}
\caption{Horizon entry. The heavy curve is the comoving Hubble radius
$1/\cH$; the coloured lines are three modes, each at its fixed comoving $1/k$. A
mode is a horizontal line because $\vk$ is a comoving label that never changes
--- what moves is the horizon, and a mode \emph{enters} where the two meet. The
mode entering exactly at matter--radiation equality defines $k_{\rm eq}$, and the
figure is drawn with it computed rather than assumed:
$\cH(a_{\rm eq}) = 0.0153\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ for $\Om = 0.31$, $h = 0.676$ and
three massless neutrino species, giving $a_{\rm eq} = 2.9\times10^{-4}$ and
$z_{\rm eq} = 3410$. At the right-hand edge $\Lambda$ takes over and $1/\cH$
turns around, so modes have begun leaving again --- the same thing inflation did,
in the same direction.}
\label{fig:horizon}
\end{figure}

Follow the potential $\Phi$ rather than the density. It is unambiguous, since
Section~\ref{sec:bridge} fixed the gauge completely, and it obeys three rules.

\emph{Outside the horizon, $\Phi$ holds still.} No causal process operates across
the mode, so nothing can reshape it. This does not follow from
\eqref{eq:newtonian_linear} --- those equations assumed $k \gg \cH$ --- and it is
not used to \emph{evolve} anything: it says there is nothing to evolve, so the
mode reaches entry still carrying the primordial amplitude of
Section~\ref{sec:primordial}. Super-horizon is where the initial condition sits,
not a phase we integrate through.

\emph{Inside the horizon during radiation domination, $\Phi$ decays.} Sub-horizon
the energy constraint is again a Poisson equation, $-k^2\Phi = 4\pi
Ga^2\bar\rho\,\delta$ --- the constraint cares only about what dominates the
density, here radiation. The \emph{dynamics} of \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear} do
not carry over: radiation has pressure, which is exactly what ``pressureless''
excluded, so how $\delta_r$ behaves is imported here rather than derived. Two
things then happen at once. The prefactor falls: $\bar\rho_r \propto a^{-4}$, so
$a^2\bar\rho_r \propto a^{-2}$. And the source refuses to grow: radiation
pressure resists compression, so $\delta_r$ does not collapse but rings, as a
sound wave in the tightly coupled photon--baryon plasma. Hence $\Phi \propto a^{-2}\times(\text{oscillation})$. Keep both factors in view. They become the two features of the spectrum: the
$a^{-2}$ envelope sets the broadband shape, and the oscillation is the BAO.

\emph{Inside the horizon during matter domination, $\Phi$ holds still again.}
Now $a^2\bar\rho_m \propto a^{-1}$ while $\delta$ grows as $D_+ \propto a$, and
the two cancel exactly. This is why the potential is constant through the whole
matter era, and why $\Phi$ stayed small in \eqref{eq:phi_small}.

Now assemble. A mode with $k < k_{\rm eq}$ enters the horizon \emph{after}
equality and so never sees the decaying phase: it arrives with its primordial
amplitude intact, $T \simeq 1$. A mode with $k > k_{\rm eq}$ enters during
radiation domination, at $a_{\rm enter}$, and decays from then until equality.
The earlier it enters, the longer it decays --- and entry is set by $k = \cH$,
with $\cH \propto 1/a$ during radiation domination, so $a_{\rm enter} \propto 1/k$. Putting the two together, the suppression a mode accumulates between entry and
equality is
\begin{equation}
T(k) \;\sim\; \left(\frac{a_{\rm enter}}{a_{\rm eq}}\right)^{2}
\;\propto\; \left(\frac{k_{\rm eq}}{k}\right)^{2} .
\label{eq:transfer_scaling}
\end{equation}


That is the shape, from horizon crossing and the Poisson equation alone: flat
below the break, falling as $k^{-2}$ above it, with the break at
$k_{\rm eq} \equiv \cH(a_{\rm eq}) \simeq 0.015\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$. It is not a
fitted parameter --- it depends on $\Om h^2$ and the radiation density --- and it
is the one scale the early universe stamps on the spectrum.

\centering

\emph{Left:} inflation supplies a featureless power law with no scale in it; the

\begin{remark}
Two refinements, neither of which changes the argument. The cold dark matter is
not quite frozen while the potential decays --- it grows logarithmically, the
\emph{M\'esz\'aros effect} --- which softens the falloff to
$T \propto k^{-2}\ln k$. You can see this in a real transfer function: fitting
the Eisenstein \& Hu form over $0.5 < k < 5\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ gives a slope of
$-1.67$, not $-2$, and the difference is the logarithm. Exercise~\ref{ex:meszaros}
works it out. And that oscillation survives as the baryon
acoustic wiggles: the plasma rings until recombination frees the photons, freezing
the wave at a single scale, the sound horizon $r_d \simeq 100\,h^{-1}$Mpc.
Baryons are only about a sixth of the matter, so the wiggles ride on the
broadband shape at the few-percent level rather than dominating it.
\end{remark}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:subhorizon}
\textbf{The other limit.} Exercise~\ref{ex:geodesic} took $v\ll1$ and produced
the force law; this one takes $k\gg\cH$ and produces the field equation.
The linearized Einstein equation that becomes Poisson is the energy constraint
\cite[Eq.~(23a)]{MaBertschinger1995},
\begin{equation*}
\underbrace{k^2\phi}_{\text{survives}}
\;+\;\underbrace{3\cH\big(\dot\phi + \cH\psi\big)}_{\text{dropped}}
\;=\; -4\pi G a^2\bar\rho\,\delta .
\end{equation*}
(i) Reducing it to the Poisson equation of \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear} needs
slightly more than $k\gg\cH$: it needs $\dot\phi$ not to be anomalously large. Assuming
$\dot\phi \lesssim \cH\phi$, which holds for the growing mode, show that the
discarded group is smaller than the retained one by
$\mathcal{O}\big((\cH/k)^2\big)$.
(ii) Put a number on it. Today $\cH_0 = H_0/c \simeq 3.3\times10^{-4}\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$.
How large is the neglected term at $k = 0.1\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$, and at
$k = 10^{-3}\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$? Which of the two lies inside the window quoted
in the Comment above?
(iii) The assumption in (i) can be \emph{removed} rather than justified:
combining the time--time constraint with the time--space one gives an algebraic
equation for $\phi$ carrying no time derivative at all, in which the correction
is manifestly $\mathcal{O}(\cH/k)$ \cite[Ex.~6.7]{DodelsonSchmidt}. Why is
eliminating an assumption preferable to bounding it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:meszaros}
\textbf{The M\'esz\'aros effect.} Take a universe of cold dark matter and
radiation, with the radiation smooth --- it does not cluster --- but dominating
the expansion until equality. Using $y \equiv a/a_{\rm eq}$ as the time
variable, the CDM density contrast obeys
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd^2\delta}{\dd y^2}
+ \frac{2+3y}{2y(1+y)}\,\frac{\dd\delta}{\dd y}
- \frac{3}{2y(1+y)}\,\delta = 0 .
\end{equation*}
(i) Verify that $\delta \propto 1 + \tfrac32 y$ solves it.
(ii) Show that this is frozen for $y\ll1$ and grows as $a$ for $y\gg1$: a mode
sitting inside the horizon during radiation domination gains almost nothing
until equality.
(iii) Section~\ref{sec:linearpk} got the suppression of $T(k)$ from the
\emph{potential} decaying while a mode waits. Here it comes from the
\emph{density} refusing to grow. Are these two effects or one, and which is
easier to turn into a number?
\end{exercise}

\subsection{The Lagrangian picture}
\label{sec:lpt}

How does gravitational collapse actually proceed? A slightly overdense region decelerates the matter around it; particles drift toward it, overshoot, and eventually orbit. The natural variable for this story is not the density at a fixed point but the trajectory of each mass element. Label each fluid element by its initial comoving position $\vq$ and track its displacement,
\begin{equation}
\vx(\vq,\tau) = \vq + \vPsi(\vq,\tau),
\qquad \vPsi(\vq,0)=0 .
\label{eq:displacement}
\end{equation}
This is Lagrangian perturbation theory (LPT). The particle equation of motion \eqref{eq:newtonian_eom} governs $\vPsi$; taking its divergence and using the Poisson equation,
\begin{equation}
\nabla_x\cdot\left[\ddot\vPsi + \cH\,\dot\vPsi\right]
= -\,\frac{3}{2}\,\Om(\tau)\,\cH^2\,\delta\big(\vx(\vq,\tau)\big) .
\label{eq:lpt_master}
\end{equation}
The density needs no equation of its own. Mass conservation, $\bar\rho\,\dd^3q = \rho(\vx)\,\dd^3x$, fixes it algebraically from the volume distortion of the map:
\begin{equation}
1 + \delta(\vx,\tau) = \frac{1}{J(\vq,\tau)},
\qquad
J = \det\left(\delta_{ij} + \Psi_{i,j}\right),
\qquad
\Psi_{i,j} \equiv \frac{\partial\Psi_i}{\partial q_j} .
\label{eq:jacobian}
\end{equation}
A volume element that shrinks ($J<1$) is overdense; one that expands is underdense. Because the determinant is nonlinear in $\vPsi$, a displacement of first order generates density perturbations at all orders: low-order LPT resums an infinite subset of Eulerian nonlinearities, the ones associated with moving matter around. The subset is real but restricted --- the Jacobian generates every power of the linear displacement, not every dynamical nonlinearity --- so more orders in density does not mean more dynamical accuracy at all scales.

At first order, \eqref{eq:lpt_master} with $\delta = -\nabla_q\cdot\vPsi^{(1)}$ (from \eqref{eq:jacobian}) says that the divergence of the displacement obeys the linear growth equation \eqref{eq:growth}. Since the flow is irrotational, $\vPsi^{(1)} = -\nabla_q\varphi^{(1)}$ for a Lagrangian potential $\varphi^{(1)}$, and matching to the Eulerian linear density gives
\begin{equation}
\nabla_q\cdot\vPsi^{(1)}(\vq,\tau) = -\,\delta^{(1)}(\vq,\tau)
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\vPsi^{(1)}(\vk,\tau) = \frac{i\vk}{k^2}\,\delta^{(1)}(\vk,\tau) .
\label{eq:za_displacement}
\end{equation}
\begin{quiz}
The density is $1+\delta = 1/J$, with no equation of its own. What happens when
$J \to 0$, and what does that correspond to physically?
\nudge{$J$ is the volume of a fluid element. What does zero volume mean for the
matter inside it, and for the map $\vq \mapsto \vx$? Try one dimension first,
with two particles approaching each other.}
\answer{$J$ is the volume of a fluid element, so $J \to 0$ is an element
crushed to zero volume: the density $1+\delta = 1/J$ diverges, and the map
$\vq \mapsto \vx$ stops being one-to-one, because two elements have arrived at
the same position. Physically this is \emph{shell crossing}, the formation of
the first caustic. It is where the fluid description fails --- a real feature of
collisionless collapse, not an artefact of the approximation --- and
Section~\ref{sec:shellcross} picks up the story there.}
\end{quiz}

\subsection{The Zel'dovich approximation}
\label{sec:za}

The \emph{Zel'dovich approximation} (ZA) \cite{Zeldovich1970} takes this linear displacement seriously beyond linear order in the density: move particles on straight lines (in comoving coordinates) and read the density off the Jacobian. Since $\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j}$ is symmetric it can be diagonalized at each $\vq$, with eigenvalues written $-D_+\lambda_1, -D_+\lambda_2, -D_+\lambda_3$, so
\begin{equation}
\delta_{\rm ZA}(\vx,\tau)
= \frac{1}{\left(1-D_+\lambda_1\right)\left(1-D_+\lambda_2\right)\left(1-D_+\lambda_3\right)} - 1 .
\label{eq:za_density}
\end{equation}

As a recipe this is completely explicit, which is why it is what initial-condition
generators do. Draw a Gaussian realization of $\delta^{(1)}(\vk)$ with variance
$\Plin(k)$ --- the output of the previous hands-on session. Get the displacement
from \eqref{eq:za_displacement}, one multiplication per mode,
$\vPsi^{(1)}(\vk) = (i\vk/k^2)\,\delta^{(1)}(\vk)$. Transform back, move every
particle from $\vq$ to $\vq + \vPsi^{(1)}$, and stop. Three Fourier transforms
and a shift, and you have a nonlinear density field.

The velocities come free. Since $\vPsi^{(1)} \propto D_+$ and nothing else in it
depends on time,
\begin{equation}
\vu_{\rm ZA} = \dot{\vPsi}^{(1)} = \frac{\dot D_+}{D_+}\,\vPsi^{(1)}
= \cH f\,\vPsi^{(1)} ,
\label{eq:za_velocity}
\end{equation}
so positions and velocities are the same field up to the factor $\cH f$ of
\eqref{eq:growth_rate}. A simulation needs both, and the ZA supplies them
together --- one of the reasons it, and its second-order extension in
Section~\ref{sec:2lpt}, are what every code uses to start.

\begin{remark}
The ZA is not a degraded linear theory, and it is worth being clear why. Linear
theory says the \emph{density} is small. The ZA instead says the
\emph{displacement} is well approximated at first order --- and then treats the
consequences exactly, through a determinant that contains every power of
$\vPsi^{(1)}$. Since bulk flows are the dominant motion on quasi-linear scales,
that is the right thing to get right. It is why the ZA follows matter into
sheets, where linear theory only says $\delta$ is growing, and why it remains a
serviceable description well past the point where $\delta = 1$.
\end{remark}
\subsection{Why a web, and not a collection of spheres}
\label{sec:web}

You have already met a model of gravitational collapse: a uniform overdense
sphere, which contracts, turns around and collapses to a point. It is the
standard toy problem, it is analytically solvable, and it is the basis of most
of what is said about halo formation.

It also essentially never happens.

Look at what a sphere requires in \eqref{eq:za_density}: all three factors must
vanish together, $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \lambda_3$. But $\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j}$ is
the second-derivative matrix of a Gaussian random field --- a random symmetric
$3\times3$ matrix --- and degenerate eigenvalues among such matrices form a set
of measure zero. Sample four hundred thousand of them and you will not find one.
\emph{Exactly spherical collapse has probability zero.}

So the generic situation is $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 > \lambda_3$, strictly, and
the three factors in \eqref{eq:za_density} vanish at three different times. That
single fact is the origin of the cosmic web. Collapse runs along the $\lambda_1$
axis first and the patch flattens into a \emph{sheet}, a Zel'dovich pancake. If
$\lambda_2 > 0$ the sheet later contracts along a second axis into a
\emph{filament}. If $\lambda_3 > 0$ as well, the filament finally draws up into a
\emph{knot}. How far a patch gets is decided by nothing more than how many of its
eigenvalues are positive --- which is fixed in the initial conditions, before any
dynamics has happened.

That makes the morphology countable. For a Gaussian field the joint distribution
of the three eigenvalues is known \cite{Doroshkevich1970}, and the volume
fractions come out as
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcl@{}}
\toprule
positive eigenvalues & fraction of volume & fate \\
\midrule
0 & $8\%$  & void, expanding in all three directions \\
1 & $42\%$ & sheet \\
2 & $42\%$ & filament \\
3 & $8\%$  & knot \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{quiz}
You know the spherical collapse model. What does a sphere require of
$\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3$, and how often does a random symmetric matrix
oblige? Then, before looking at the numbers: of void, sheet, filament and knot,
which two should be commonest, and why should the table be symmetric?
\nudge{For the patch to stay spherical, how must the three eigenvalues be
related? Now write down any $3\times3$ symmetric matrix off the top of your head
--- were two of its eigenvalues equal? Would you ever expect them to be? For the
second half, treat each eigenvalue's sign as a coin flip and count.}
\answer{A sphere needs $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \lambda_3$, and for a random
symmetric matrix that is a probability-zero coincidence: no matrix you write
down will oblige. So spherical collapse describes an event that never happens,
and generic collapse is anisotropic --- it runs fastest along the largest
eigenvalue, forming a sheet first, then a filament, then a knot. For the
fractions: if the three eigenvalue signs were independent coin flips, the split
would be $12.5/37.5/37.5/12.5$ per cent. The true split, $8/42/42/8$, keeps the
symmetry --- a Gaussian field has no preferred sign of $\delta$ --- but shifts
weight toward sheets and filaments, because the eigenvalues are not independent:
they repel.}

Read the middle rows: \textbf{$84\%$ of the volume is headed for a sheet or a
filament, and only $8\%$ ever collapses along all three axes.} The web is not
decoration draped over a population of spherical halos. It is what almost
everything becomes, and the compact objects are the rare case.

\centering

\label{fig:web}
\end{figure}

This is also why the ZA is worth more than its accuracy suggests. It predicts the
skeleton of the cosmic web --- where the sheets and filaments will be --- from
the second derivatives of the initial potential alone, with no dynamics
whatsoever. The hands-on session that follows this lecture puts that to the test:
you will displace a Gaussian field once, classify each particle by how many of its
eigenvalues are collapsing, and get a web out.

\subsection{Shell crossing, and where the approximation dies}
\label{sec:shellcross}

The first of those collapses is also the end of the theory. When
$D_+\lambda_1 \to 1$ the first factor in \eqref{eq:za_density} vanishes, the
density formally diverges, and $J \to 0$. That moment is \emph{shell crossing}:
particle trajectories intersect, the map $\vq \mapsto \vx$ stops being
invertible, several streams with different velocities occupy the same point, and
both the single-stream description of Section~\ref{sec:vlasov} and the Zel'dovich
solution itself cease to be valid.

Be clear, then, about what the later stages of Fig.~\ref{fig:collapse} are.
Formally the second factor vanishes later wherever $\lambda_2>0$ and the third
later still wherever $\lambda_3>0$, and the sequence sheet $\rightarrow$ filament
$\rightarrow$ knot is that ordering of eigenvalue crossings. As a guide to
\emph{morphology} it is excellent, and it is what the table above counts. As
\emph{dynamics} past the first caustic it is an extrapolation: a Zel'dovich knot
is not a halo, and the approximation says nothing about binding, virialization,
or the stable orbiting configuration that collapsed matter actually settles into.
Getting through shell crossing needs adhesion models, phase-space methods, or
$N$-body simulation --- which is precisely why the initial conditions of
Section~\ref{sec:2lpt} matter, since a simulation is what you hand them to.

\subsection{Second order: 2LPT and initial conditions}
\label{sec:2lpt}

At second order the displacement responds to the gravitational tide generated at first order. Expanding \eqref{eq:lpt_master} and \eqref{eq:jacobian} to second order, the divergence $\Psi^{(2)}_{i,i}$ obeys the linear-growth operator with a quadratic source \cite[Eqs.~(E.30)--(E.31)]{Jeong2010}; separating variables, the second-order growth factor obeys
\begin{equation}
\ddot D_2 + \cH\dot D_2 - \frac{3}{2}\Om\cH^2 D_2
= -\frac{3}{2}\Om\cH^2 D_+^2 ,
\qquad
D_2(\tau) \simeq -\frac{3}{7}\,D_+^2(\tau)\;\Om^{-1/143},
\label{eq:D2}
\end{equation}
where the fit is accurate to $0.6\%$ for flat \lcdm{} and the EdS limit is $D_2 = -\tfrac37 D_+^2$. (This Lagrangian $D_2$ is a different object from the second-order amplitude of Eulerian perturbation theory \cite{EFTnotes}; the literature uses the same symbol for both.) The spatial source is the second principal invariant of the deformation tensor $\varphi^{(1)}_{,ij}$: writing $\vPsi^{(2)} = \nabla_q\varphi^{(2)}$,
\begin{equation}
\nabla_q^2\varphi^{(2)}
\simeq -\frac{3}{7}\,\Om^{-1/143}
\sum_{i>j}\Big[\varphi^{(1)}_{,ii}\,\varphi^{(1)}_{,jj} - \big(\varphi^{(1)}_{,ij}\big)^2\Big],
\label{eq:2lpt_potential}
\end{equation}
with $\vPsi^{(1)} = -\nabla_q\varphi^{(1)}$ and $\nabla^2_q\varphi^{(1)} = \delta^{(1)}$, both understood as full-time fields with their growth factors included. Do not read \eqref{eq:2lpt_potential} as a purely tidal source: the invariant decomposes into an isotropic piece built from $\delta^2$ and a trace-free tidal piece, and it is nonzero even for a spherical patch, as Exercise~\ref{ex:d2} makes explicit. The full 2LPT map, including velocities, is
\begin{equation}
\vx = \vq - \nabla_q\varphi^{(1)} + \nabla_q\varphi^{(2)},
\qquad
\vu = -\cH f_1 \nabla_q\varphi^{(1)} + \cH f_2\nabla_q\varphi^{(2)},
\qquad
f_2 \simeq 2\,\Om^{6/11} ,
\label{eq:2lpt_map}
\end{equation}
where $f_1 \equiv \dd\ln D_+/\dd\ln a = f$ and, because the convention here has $D_2<0$ so that $\ln D_2$ is undefined,
\begin{equation}
f_2 \equiv \frac{\dd\ln|D_2|}{\dd\ln a} = \frac{\dot D_2}{\cH D_2} .
\label{eq:f2_def}
\end{equation}
The velocity in \eqref{eq:2lpt_map} is unaffected by this: the sign of $D_2$ already sits in $\varphi^{(2)}$. Physically: Zel'dovich streams are straight; the tide bends them. 2LPT is the standard for setting up initial conditions of $N$-body simulations: starting from the ZA alone leaves slowly decaying transients, and 2LPT strongly suppresses them. How large the residual is depends on the starting redshift, the force resolution, and which statistic you look at \cite[App.~E.5]{Jeong2010}.

\begin{remark}
Sign conventions differ across the literature, so it is worth saying which one
this is. Everything in this lecture --- the displacement $\vx = \vq + \vPsi$, the
Jacobian, the deformation tensor and its eigenvalues, the growth equation for
$D_2$ and its fit, the second-order source, and the map \eqref{eq:2lpt_map}
itself --- follows Jeong \cite[App.~E]{Jeong2010} exactly, including $D_2 < 0$
with $+\nabla_q\varphi^{(2)}$ in the map. Formulae carry across in either
direction without adjustment. Other references absorb the minus sign into $D_2$
instead; check before comparing codes.
\end{remark}

Do the Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions carry the same information?
Order by order, and before shell crossing, yes --- the companion notes
\cite{EFTnotes} make the correspondence explicit by recovering the Eulerian
kernels from the displacement map.

\subsection{Galaxies: linear bias, and where this goes next}
\label{sec:bias}

Everything so far has been about matter. Surveys measure galaxies, and galaxies
are not matter.

They form in the deepest potential wells, so they are not a fair sample of the
density field but a \emph{biased} one. The simplest statement of that --- and the
only one this course needs --- is that on large scales the galaxy overdensity is
proportional to the matter overdensity,
\begin{equation}
\delta_g(\vx) = b_1\,\delta(\vx) ,
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
P_g(k) = b_1^2\,\Plin(k) ,
\label{eq:linear_bias}
\end{equation}
with a single number $b_1$, the \emph{linear bias}. It exceeds one for the
massive halos that host bright galaxies, because a rare peak responds more than
proportionately to the large-scale density it sits in: raise the background a
little and the number of regions crossing the threshold to form a galaxy rises a
lot. Nothing about $b_1$ is predicted by the theory in these lectures. It is
measured alongside the cosmology.

\begin{quiz}
Why should $b_1$ be greater than one? Galaxies are made of matter, so why do they
not simply trace it?
\nudge{Galaxies form where the density crosses a threshold. Add a long-wavelength
mode that lifts the background slightly across a large region: the density barely
moved, but how much did the \emph{number of places above threshold} move? Sketch
a Gaussian with a cut near its tail and slide it right.}
\answer{Thresholds amplify. Galaxies form only where the density exceeds a
threshold that sits in the tail of the distribution. Now add a small
long-wavelength overdensity: the density field barely changes, but the number
of regions above the threshold changes by much more, because shifting a
Gaussian slightly moves a large fraction of its tail across a fixed cut. So
galaxy counts respond more strongly than the matter does, which is $b_1 > 1$
--- and the rarer the object, the stronger the amplification, which is why the
most massive galaxies are the most biased. (This argument is the
\emph{peak--background split}, and it reappears whenever bias is derived
properly.)}
\end{quiz}

Two things are worth knowing about \eqref{eq:linear_bias}. It is the first term
of an expansion, not a law --- at smaller scales the response stops being linear,
picks up a dependence on the tidal field, and acquires a stochastic piece. And it
is written in real space, whereas a survey infers radial positions from
redshifts, so peculiar velocities distort what is measured along the line of
sight. Both corrections, and the one-loop machinery that organises them, are the
subject of the companion notes \cite{EFTnotes}.

\begin{remark}
That closes the loop the course opened. Section~\ref{sec:linearpk} showed how to
get $\Plin(k)$ from a cosmological model, and it has since been put to two
distinct uses. Fed through the displacement map of this lecture it becomes
initial conditions for an $N$-body simulation, which is what the hands-on session
does. Multiplied by $b_1^2$ it becomes a prediction for what a galaxy survey
measures. One spectrum, two roads: the first leads to simulations of structure
formation, the second to cosmological inference from galaxy clustering.
\end{remark}

Solve \eqref{eq:D2} in EdS ($\cH = 2/\tau$, $\Om=1$, $D_+ = a \propto \tau^2$) and verify $D_2 = -\tfrac37 a^2$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:1dza}
\textbf{(Optional.)} In one spatial dimension the Zel'dovich approximation is exact until shell crossing. Prove this by showing that the 1D displacement $\Psi^{(1)}$ solves the full nonlinear equation of motion, using the fact that in 1D the force on a sheet depends only on the mass on either side of it. Assume plane-parallel sheets, a subtracted homogeneous background, and boundary conditions (periodic, or a fixed reference point) that determine the constant in $\int\delta\,\dd x$.
\end{exercise}
