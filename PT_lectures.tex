\medskip
\noindent\textbf{The setting and conventions.} Fixed for the whole course: a
spatially flat Friedmann--Lema\^itre--Robertson--Walker (FLRW) universe ---
homogeneous, isotropic and expanding --- carrying \emph{scalar} perturbations
only. The three perturbation types decouple at linear order: vectors decay, and
tensors --- gravitational waves --- do not source density perturbations, so
structure formation is a scalar story. The unperturbed metric is
\begin{equation*}
\dd s^2 = a^2(\tau)\left[-\dd\tau^2 + \delta_{ij}\,\dd x^i\dd x^j\right],
\end{equation*}
written in comoving coordinates $\vx$, which ride along with the expansion: a
galaxy at rest keeps the same $\vx$ while its physical separation from us,
$a\vx$, grows. Here $\tau$ is \emph{conformal} time, defined by
$\dd t = a\,\dd\tau$, so that $\tau = \int\dd t/a$: an integral, not $t/a$.
Overdots mean $\partial/\partial\tau$ throughout --- at fixed comoving position
for a field, at fixed element for a trajectory --- and
$\cH \equiv \dd\ln a/\dd\tau = aH$ is the expansion rate in that time. Spatial
indices are raised and lowered with $\delta_{ij}$, so a repeated pair is summed
regardless of placement.
\begin{reading}
\bait{Why measure time with $\tau$ rather than with $t$?}
Conformal time is used because of the form the metric then takes: with $\dd\tau$
and $\dd\vx$ appearing symmetrically, radial light rays obey
$\dd x = \pm\dd\tau$, so light travels at unit comoving speed and an interval of
conformal time \emph{is} a comoving distance. That is what will make the horizon
a clean notion in Section~\ref{sec:linearpk}.
\end{reading}

Structure is what happens when this is not quite true. The metric
picks up small perturbations, and the matter density departs from its mean by
\begin{equation*}
\delta(\vx,\tau) \equiv \frac{\rho(\vx,\tau) - \bar\rho(\tau)}{\bar\rho(\tau)} ,
\end{equation*}
with $|\delta|\ll1$ early on and of order unity by the time there are galaxies to
count. How $\delta$ gets from one to the other is the whole course.

\begin{derivation}
Show, from the definition above, that the density perturbation
$\delta\rho \equiv \rho - \bar\rho$ averages to zero at every time,
$\overline{\delta\rho}(\tau) = 0$, and hence that $\delta(\vk{=}0) = 0$.
Nothing about gravity or expansion is needed, and that is the lesson to bring
back: which object in the split $\rho = \bar\rho\,(1+\delta)$ carries the mean?
(The hands-on session sets the $\vk = 0$ mode to zero by hand for exactly this
reason.)
\end{derivation}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Position space and Fourier space.} Both hold the same
information. In position space $\delta(\vx)$ is a lumpy field, where intuition lives, and where Lecture~2's collapsing sheets are easiest to picture.
In Fourier space we resolve it into plane waves.

Start in a periodic box of side $L$, which is what the hands-on session uses.
The field is a discrete sum, one term per mode,
\begin{equation*}
\delta(\vx) = \sum_{\vk}\,\delta_{\vk}\,e^{i\vk\cdot\vx} ,
\qquad
\vk = \frac{2\pi}{L}\,\bm{n} , \quad \bm{n} \in \mathbb{Z}^3 ,
\end{equation*}
with the spacing $2\pi/L$ set by the box. Let $L \to \infty$: the spacing closes
up, the sum becomes an integral, and we have the convention used throughout,
\begin{equation*}
\delta(\vk) = \int \dd^3x\, e^{-i\vk\cdot\vx}\,\delta(\vx),
\qquad
\delta(\vx) = \int_{\vk} e^{i\vk\cdot\vx}\,\delta(\vk),
\qquad
\int_{\vk} \equiv \int\!\frac{\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3} .
\end{equation*}
Each mode is a plane wave of comoving wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/k$, so large
$k$ means \emph{small} scales: $k = 0.1\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ is
$\lambda \simeq 63\,h^{-1}$Mpc, a few cluster diameters.

Why bother: because the background is homogeneous, the linearized equations have
coefficients independent of position. A plane wave stays a plane wave, and each
$\delta(\vk)$ evolves independently of every other: one partial differential
equation in three dimensions becomes one ordinary differential equation per
$\vk$. That is what makes linear theory solvable.
Figure~\ref{fig:fourier} draws it.

\begin{figure}[t]

One warning, because the picture invites the wrong conclusion. The longest wave

reaches them first, and why almost everything difficult in this course happens

\subsection{The Newtonian equations and their assumptions}
\label{sec:bridge}

From here on everything is Newtonian: a fluid in an expanding box, no metric in
sight. Three equations do all the work, and they rest on one gauge choice and
four numbered assumptions, A1--A3. Keep the list in a corner of the board: the
assumptions do different jobs, fail at different times, and exactly one will be
dropped in Lecture~2.

\paragraph{The gauge choice.} The arena was fixed in the prelude: flat FLRW,
scalar perturbations only. What comes before any assumption is a \emph{choice},
and it restricts no physics. Fix the gauge completely by writing the
perturbed metric in conformal Newtonian (longitudinal) form
\cite{MaBertschinger1995},
\begin{equation}
\dd s^2 = a^2(\tau)\left[-(1+2\psi)\,\dd\tau^2
+ (1-2\phi)\,\delta_{ij}\,\dd x^i\dd x^j\right] .
\label{eq:newtonian_gauge}
\end{equation}
Here $\psi(\vx,\tau)$ perturbs $g_{00}$ --- it is what a slow particle falls
down --- and $\phi(\vx,\tau)$ perturbs the spatial metric --- it is what appears
in the field equation. Both are fields on space and time, exactly like $\delta$:
they vary from place to place, which is what makes them perturbations, and they
evolve, which is what the rest of this lecture computes.

\begin{reading}
\bait{Why the factors of $2$? And why a minus sign on $\phi$ but a plus on
$\psi$?}
Both are conventions, and both are chosen to keep every later formula clean.
The $2$ normalizes $\psi$: with it, $\Gamma^i{}_{00} = \partial_i\psi$ exactly at
first order, so a slow particle obeys $\ddot x^i = -\partial_i\psi$ and $\psi$
\emph{is} the Newtonian potential rather than twice it
(Exercise~\ref{ex:geodesic}). Write $1+\psi$ instead and you get
$\Gamma^i{}_{00} = \tfrac12\partial_i\psi$, so a factor of one half propagates
into Newton's law and into the Poisson equation and never leaves. The relative
minus sign does the analogous job further down: it is what makes vanishing
anisotropic stress read $\phi = \psi$, rather than $\phi = -\psi$, so that the
two potentials can be replaced by the single $\Phi$.
\end{reading}
\paragraph{A1: weak fields, $|\phi|,\,|\psi| \ll 1$.} Work to first order in
both potentials. An observation as much as an assumption: the potentials
are of order $10^{-5}$ on every scale we will meet, at every time. Note what A1
does \emph{not} mention: the density contrast $\delta$.

\paragraph{A2: cold matter.} The matter is cold dark matter and baryons, pressureless and slow, $u \ll 1$. Newtonian gravity has one
potential, not two, and A2 is what closes the gap: the traceless part of
Einstein's equations sources $\phi - \psi$ through anisotropic stress alone,
which cold matter does not carry, so for us the two potentials coincide,
$\phi = \psi \equiv \Phi$.
\begin{reading}
\bait{I said cold matter carries no anisotropic stress. Is there anything in the
universe that does?}
The fine print: it is the \emph{total} anisotropic stress that must vanish
\cite[Eq.~(23d)]{MaBertschinger1995}, and free-streaming neutrinos spoil it at
the $15\%$ level during radiation domination. That contribution fades as matter
comes to dominate, so in the matter era $\phi = \psi$ is accurate.
\end{reading}

\paragraph{A3: sub-horizon modes. A4: linear contrasts.} The last two arrive
with the system itself: keep only $k \gg \cH$ (A3), and linearize in the
density contrast, $|\delta| \ll 1$ (A4). They look alike and are not: A3 is
permanent, while A4 is temporary: the one assumption Lecture~2 drops, for a
reason given at the end of this section.

A3 deserves a word, since we lean on it constantly. Because $\cH$ is a rate per
unit conformal time, $1/\cH$ is a \emph{time}, roughly how long the universe
takes to expand appreciably; and because light travels at unit comoving speed in
conformal time, the same number is also a \emph{length}, the comoving distance
light covers in that time. That length is the \emph{comoving Hubble radius},
loosely ``the horizon''. A mode is \emph{sub-horizon} when $k \gg \cH$ ---
shorter than the horizon, so signals cross it many times while the universe
expands once, and gravity acts coherently across it --- and \emph{super-horizon}
when $k \ll \cH$, where nothing crosses it at all. Section~\ref{sec:linearpk} is
about what happens to a mode when it changes from one to the other.

\medskip
\noindent With the list complete, here is the whole of what follows in one
sentence: \textbf{we are describing a self-gravitating fluid, matter moving in a potential that it sources itself.} That alone fixes what the system must
contain. A fluid conserves mass and momentum, which is two equations; and
because the gravity is its own, a third is needed to tie the potential back to
the density. Those three, one at a time.

\emph{Mass conservation.} The contrast changes only because matter flows. For
the peculiar velocity $\vu \equiv \dd\vx/\dd\tau$, mass conservation reads
\begin{equation}
\dot\delta + \nabla\cdot\left[(1+\delta)\,\vu\right] = 0 ,
\label{eq:continuity}
\end{equation}
in which dilution by the expansion never appears; it is already inside
$\bar\rho$, which is the content of Derivation~1. At linear order (A4) the
product term drops and \eqref{eq:continuity} becomes $\dot\delta = -\theta$,
with $\theta \equiv \nabla\cdot\vu$. (The relativistic version carries one more
term, a dilation $3\dot\phi$; A3 suppresses it, as
Exercise~\ref{ex:subhorizon} shows.)

\emph{Newton's second law.} For a pressureless fluid (A2) there is no separate Euler
equation to import: Euler \emph{is} Newton's second law applied to fluid
elements. Follow one element, so that $\vx(\tau)$ is its trajectory. To first
order in $\Phi$ (A1),
\begin{equation}
\ddot\vx + \cH\,\dot\vx = -\nabla\Phi .
\label{eq:newtonian_eom}
\end{equation}
Since $\vu = \dot\vx$, this \emph{is} the fluid's momentum equation,
$\dot\vu + \cH\vu = -\nabla\Phi$, written per element rather than per point. The density cannot appear in it, because momentum conservation is a statement
about motion. Eliminate $\vu$ and $\Phi$ between the three members and you get a
single equation for $\delta$ alone; that is \eqref{eq:growth}, and
Section~\ref{sec:vlasov} does it.

The new term is the drag $\cH\dot\vx$, and it is not friction: set $\Phi = 0$
and \eqref{eq:newtonian_eom} says $\dot\vx \propto 1/a$: free particles coast
to rest in comoving coordinates as their peculiar velocities redshift away.
Two exercises reach it from opposite directions:
Exercise~\ref{ex:geodesic} comes down from the geodesic equation of
\eqref{eq:newtonian_gauge}, and Exercise~\ref{ex:eomnewton} builds up from
Newtonian momentum conservation without relativity at all. Lecture~2 is built on this equation: it follows
elements rather than fields, and that change of variable is the whole of the
Lagrangian picture. For now we need only its divergence: with $\vu = \dot\vx$
and $\theta = \nabla\cdot\vu$, at linear order (A4)
$\dot\theta = -\cH\,\theta - \nabla^2\Phi$.

\emph{The Poisson equation.} What closes the system is the field equation, and
here A3 does its work: every term carrying a factor of $\cH$
where its neighbours carry $k$ is suppressed by $(\cH/k)^2$, and the energy
constraint of general relativity collapses to the Poisson equation,
$\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi Ga^2\bar\rho\,\delta = \tfrac32\Om(\tau)\cH^2\delta$, the
last equality by the Friedmann equation. Exercise~\ref{ex:subhorizon}
carries out the reduction and puts a number on what was dropped.

Assembled, at linear order (A4):
\begin{equation}
\dot\delta = -\theta,
\qquad
\dot\theta = -\cH\,\theta - \nabla^2\Phi,
\qquad
\nabla^2\Phi = \frac{3}{2}\Om(\tau)\cH^2\delta .
\label{eq:newtonian_linear}
\end{equation}
Ma \& Bertschinger \cite[\S 5]{MaBertschinger1995} give the unapproximated
system; Dodelson \& Schmidt \cite[Ch.~12]{DodelsonSchmidt} set out this same
starting point with its assumptions listed.

\begin{derivation}\label{der:system}
Assemble \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear} for yourself: linearize
\eqref{eq:continuity} to get the first member, take the divergence of
\eqref{eq:newtonian_eom} to get the second, and write down the third. Keep
track of which assumption each step spends. The Comment below is the answer,
so try it before reading on.
\end{derivation}

\begin{reading}
\bait{We used mass conservation and momentum conservation. Where is energy
conservation?}
Three answers, which are one answer seen from three sides.

For cold matter, mass conservation \emph{is} energy conservation. The energy
density is rest mass; the kinetic part is smaller by $u^2 \ll 1$ (A2). So
\eqref{eq:continuity} already did that job.

There is also no independent energy equation available to write. A fluid with pressure needs one, since something must say how the pressure evolves, and that is
what closes the system. Coldness closes it instead: with no velocity dispersion
the chain of moment equations truncates after Euler, which is why two fluid
equations suffice here where a gas would need three. This is also the assumption
that expires. Once orbits cross, one velocity per point is no longer true and
the truncation fails, which is where Lecture~2 stops.

And least obviously, you did use it. The relativistic statement that energy
density curves spacetime is the time--time Einstein equation, called the \emph{energy constraint}, and its sub-horizon limit is the third member of
\eqref{eq:newtonian_linear}. Poisson \emph{is} the energy equation, in the only
form general relativity offers; Exercise~\ref{ex:subhorizon} works with it
unapproximated.
\end{reading}

\begin{quiz}
Gravity is weak \emph{everywhere} --- $10^{-5}$ on every scale --- so A1 costs
us nothing. Why do we need A3 as well? What fails if you apply
\eqref{eq:newtonian_linear} to a super-horizon mode?
\nudge{A super-horizon mode is weakly gravitating too. So point at the one
member of \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear} that still fails there.}
\answer{Poisson fails. It ties $\Phi$ at one point to the density everywhere on
the same time slice, instantaneously, and that makes sense only if light crosses
the scale of interest in far less than an expansion time. That is A3, and a
super-horizon mode fails it however weak gravity is: no signal has crossed the
mode even once, so there is no settled potential to describe. A1 linearizes the
metric on every scale; A3 is what buys Newtonian dynamics, and only inside the
horizon.}
\end{quiz}

\begin{remark}
Which assumption lives where. The three members of \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear}
do not lean on the same legs. \emph{Continuity} is the most robust: mass
conservation, needing only A4 to linearize and one use of A3, to drop the
relativistic dilation term $3\dot\phi$. \emph{Euler}, in the form
$\dot\theta = -\cH\theta - \nabla^2\Phi$, needs A2 and A1 --- cold, slow matter
in a weak field --- but \emph{no} use of A3 at all: for cold matter it is exact
at linear order in this gauge. \emph{Poisson} is where A3 does its real work:
the relativistic energy constraint carries extra terms of relative size
$(\cH/k)^2$, and dropping them is what turns a constraint of general relativity into Newton's field equation. Exercise~\ref{ex:subhorizon} makes this
quantitative. And the $\phi = \psi$ of A2 underwrites the system as a whole
rather than any single member: Euler feels the time--time potential while
Poisson sources the spatial one.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{The smallness of $\Phi$.} A3 has a second consequence, and the
whole of Lecture~2 rests on it.

\emph{It keeps the metric linear.} Inverting the Poisson equation,
\begin{equation}
|\Phi| \sim \frac{3}{2}\,\Om\left(\frac{\cH}{k}\right)^{\!2}\delta ,
\label{eq:phi_small}
\end{equation}
the potential is the density contrast suppressed by two powers of $\cH/k$. At
$k = 0.1\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ that factor is about $10^{-5}$, so even a patch with
$\delta \sim 100$ carries $|\Phi| \sim 5\times10^{-4}$. Gravity stays weak
precisely where clustering becomes strong, which is the licence for everything
that follows. We will let $\delta$ grow past unity and resum it to all orders
through displacements, while never going beyond linear order in the metric. Those
two statements sound contradictory and are not: $\Phi$ and $\delta$ are separated
by $(\cH/k)^2$, and it is $\delta$ alone that becomes large. Dropping A4
leaves A1 intact.

\begin{derivation}
Derive \eqref{eq:phi_small} yourself: write the third member of
\eqref{eq:newtonian_linear} in Fourier space and show that
$|\Phi| = \tfrac32\,\Om(\tau)\,(\cH/k)^2\,|\delta|$, so that sub-horizon (A3)
the weak-field condition (A1) on $\Phi$ is guaranteed rather than assumed.
Then put in numbers --- $\cH_0 \simeq 3.3\times10^{-4}\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$,
$k = 0.1\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$, $\delta = 100$ --- and check the $5\times10^{-4}$
quoted above.
\end{derivation}

\begin{reading}
\bait{I keep saying $\Phi$ is unambiguous. Is $\delta$?}
\emph{It also makes $\delta$ meaningful.} Here is where the trouble comes from,
since it is not obvious. Writing $\delta = (\rho - \bar\rho)/\bar\rho$ requires
$\bar\rho$ \emph{at the same time} as $\rho$, and in general relativity
``the same time'' is a choice of how to slice spacetime into spatial surfaces,
not a fact about the universe. Shift that slicing infinitesimally,
$\tau \to \tau + \xi^0(\vx,\tau)$. Since $\bar\rho$ depends on time it moves
too, by $\dot{\bar\rho}\,\xi^0$, and the background continuity equation
$\dot{\bar\rho} = -3\cH(1+w)\bar\rho$ then gives
\begin{equation*}
\delta \;\longrightarrow\; \delta + 3\cH(1+w)\,\xi^0 .
\end{equation*}
That is the whole statement: $\delta$ changes when you re-slice, so it is not by
itself a physical quantity. It is also why the same mode grows as $(k\tau)^2$ in
synchronous gauge and sits frozen in the Newtonian gauge until horizon entry
\cite[\S 7]{MaBertschinger1995}, and why ``do perturbations grow outside the
horizon?'' has no gauge-independent answer.

There is a repair, and it is worth seeing because it explains A3 from a second
direction. The combination
$\Delta \equiv \delta + 3\cH(1+w)\,v$, with $v$ the velocity potential
($\vu = \nabla v$), is gauge-invariant --- the shift above cancels against the
one $v$ picks up --- and it obeys
$-k^2\Phi = \tfrac32\Om\cH^2\Delta$ \emph{exactly}, on all scales, with no
sub-horizon limit anywhere. Our Poisson equation is that one with $\Delta$
replaced by $\delta$. The error is the difference: $\theta \sim \cH\delta$ gives
$v \sim \cH\delta/k^2$, so
$\Delta - \delta \sim (\cH/k)^2\,\delta$. Sub-horizon the two agree and $\delta$
is as good as gauge-invariant; super-horizon the correction is the whole answer.

$\Phi$ carries no such disease: \eqref{eq:newtonian_gauge} fixes the slicing
completely, leaving no residual freedom, so $\Phi$ is physical on \emph{every} scale: it is the Bardeen potential under another name. That is exactly why
Section~\ref{sec:linearpk} follows $\Phi$ and not $\delta$ when it has to cross
the horizon.
\end{reading}


\begin{remark}
Where relativity actually enters. Everything these lectures \emph{compute} is
Newtonian and sub-horizon: the system \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear}, the linear
growth it implies, and the whole of Lecture~2. Beyond the FLRW background
itself, general relativity appears exactly three times, each as an input rather
than a calculation: the metric \eqref{eq:newtonian_gauge} with $\phi = \psi$,
which defines $\Phi$; the primordial spectrum of Section~\ref{sec:primordial},
which is inflation's output; and the one super-horizon fact that $\Phi$ holds
still outside the horizon (Section~\ref{sec:linearpk}), used only to carry the
initial condition intact to horizon entry. One dynamical ingredient is quoted
rather than derived: the acoustic oscillation of the radiation fluid, which
needs pressure and so lies outside \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear}. Everything else
is the three equations above.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The pressureless fluid, and how perturbations grow}
\label{sec:vlasov}

Everything needed is already in \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear}. One check first:
the curl of \eqref{eq:newtonian_eom} at linear order gives
$\dot{\bm{w}} + \cH\,\bm{w} = 0$ for the vorticity $\bm{w} = \nabla\times\vu$,
so rotation decays as $1/a$ and stays zero if it starts zero, so the velocity field is fully specified by its divergence $\theta$. Now combine all three:
differentiate $\dot\delta = -\theta$, substitute $\dot\theta$, and eliminate
$\nabla^2\Phi$ with Poisson. No coefficient in the result carries a $k$, so the
position dependence factors out and every mode grows by one common factor,
\begin{equation}
\ddot D + \cH\,\dot D - \frac{3}{2}\Om(\tau)\,\cH^2 D = 0 .
\label{eq:growth}
\end{equation}
The growing solution $D_+(\tau)$ defines linear growth, $\delta^{(1)}(\vx,\tau) = D_+(\tau)\,\delta_0(\vx)$; in Einstein--de Sitter (EdS, $\Om = 1$) $D_+ = a$ and the decaying mode is $a^{-3/2}$. The velocity follows from continuity, and introduces the growth rate $f$,
\begin{equation}
\theta^{(1)} = -\cH f\,\delta^{(1)},
\qquad
f \equiv \frac{\dd\ln D_+}{\dd\ln a}
\simeq \Om^{5/9}(\tau) \quad\text{(flat \lcdm)} ,
\label{eq:growth_rate}
\end{equation}
with $f = 1$ in EdS. The often-quoted exponent $0.55$~\cite{Linder2005} agrees
with $5/9$ at the sub-percent level over viable cosmologies.

Exercise~\ref{ex:eds} gets both solutions by hand in Einstein--de Sitter, which
is the case worth doing, since everything else is a numerical perturbation on
it.

Two modes, so the general solution mixes them:
$\delta = A(\vx)\,D_+(\tau) + B(\vx)\,D_-(\tau)$, with $A$ and $B$ fixed by the
initial density \emph{and} the initial velocity, since a second-order equation needs
two pieces of initial data. Relative to the growing mode the second term falls
as $D_-/D_+ \propto a^{-5/2}$, so after enough expansion only $A(\vx)D_+(\tau)$
survives. That is what licenses writing $\delta^{(1)} = D_+(\tau)\,\delta_0(\vx)$
above: one function of position times one of time, rather than two.

Be careful what this does \emph{not} erase. It does not erase the initial
conditions: $A(\vx)$ is the primordial field, remembered exactly, and computing
it is the whole business of Lecture~1. What is forgotten is only the other half of the initial data: whatever part of the start did not lie on the growing
solution. This is why initial conditions for a simulation can be laid down using
the growing mode alone (Section~\ref{sec:2lpt}), and why a slightly wrong start
relaxes onto the right answer instead of propagating.

The growing mode is remarkable for how \emph{slow} it is. In a static medium an
overdensity grows exponentially; expansion converts that runaway into a power
law. The Friedmann equation makes $\cH^2 \sim Ga^2\bar\rho$, so the drag
$\cH\dot D$ and the source $\tfrac32\Om\cH^2 D$ in \eqref{eq:growth} are always
comparable: gravity wins, but only just. That is why
perturbations of order $10^{-3}$ at recombination are of order unity now, after a
factor of a thousand in $a$, instead of having collapsed long ago, and why
perturbation theory is useful across five decades of expansion.

\subsection{Gaussian random fields and the power spectrum}
\label{sec:stats}

Inflation delivers, to very good approximation, Gaussian initial conditions: the linear field $\delta^{(1)}(\vk)$ is a Gaussian random field, meaning that any finite set of its Fourier modes is jointly Gaussian with zero mean. Such a field is characterized completely by its power spectrum,
\begin{equation}
\langle \delta^{(1)}(\vk)\,\delta^{(1)}(\vk')\rangle = (2\pi)^3\dirac(\vk+\vk')\,\Plin(k) ,
\label{eq:plin_def}
\end{equation}
where two distinct assumptions are at work. Statistical \emph{homogeneity} --- invariance under translations --- is what forces the delta function, so that modes whose wavevectors do not sum to zero are uncorrelated. Statistical \emph{isotropy} is a separate statement, invariance under rotations, and it is what makes $\Plin$ a function of the magnitude $k=|\vk|$ alone rather than of the vector $\vk$.

\begin{reading}
\bait{We just defined $\Plin$ in Fourier space. What is it in position space?}
The two-point correlation function: the excess probability, over random, of
finding matter at two points separated by $\vr$,
\begin{equation*}
\xi(r) \equiv \big\langle \delta(\vx)\,\delta(\vx+\vr)\big\rangle
= \int_{\vk} \Plin(k)\,e^{i\vk\cdot\vr}
= \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int_0^\infty \dd k\; k^2\,\Plin(k)\,\frac{\sin kr}{kr} ,
\end{equation*}
the last step doing the angular integral for an isotropic spectrum. So
$\xi$ and $\Plin$ are a Fourier pair and carry identical information, the same statement as the prelude's, one level up: a field can be held in position
space or in Fourier space, and so can its statistics.

Two things follow. At zero separation $\xi(0) = \langle\delta^2\rangle =
\int_{\vk}\Plin(k)$, the variance of the field, but treat that with care.
Because the power per logarithmic interval $k^3\Plin/2\pi^2$ \emph{rises} with
$k$, the integral is dominated by the smallest scales and keeps growing as you
push the upper limit out; an unqualified variance is not a number. This is
precisely why amplitudes are quoted smoothed, which is what the window in
\eqref{eq:sigma8} is for. On a grid the upper limit is the Nyquist frequency and
the integral is finite, which is what lets the hands-on session check its
normalization against it. And the baryon wiggles of
Section~\ref{sec:linearpk} become a single bump: an oscillation of period
$2\pi/r_d$ in $\Plin$ transforms into one feature in $\xi$ at $r = r_d \simeq
100\,h^{-1}$Mpc. Surveys quote whichever is more convenient; it is the same
physics either way.
\end{reading}

\begin{reading}
\bait{How would you actually generate one of these fields on a computer?}
Note the one pair that is exempt: $\vk' = -\vk$, where the delta function fires. Since the field is real, $\delta(-\vk) = \delta(\vk)^*$, so that case is $\langle|\delta(\vk)|^2\rangle$: a variance, and necessarily non-negative. It is not a counterexample to ``uncorrelated'' but the definition of $\Plin$ itself.

\paragraph{Generating a realization.} That a Gaussian field is fixed completely by $\Plin$ is a
practical statement as much as a formal one, and it is what the hands-on session
begins with. On a periodic grid the recipe is three lines:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item for each mode $\vk$, draw a complex amplitude with zero mean and variance
$\Plin(k)/V$, where $V$ is the box volume --- equivalently, draw the modulus
from a Rayleigh distribution and the phase uniformly on $[0,2\pi)$. That volume
factor is the step that most often goes wrong, and it fails silently: the field
looks right and every number computed from it is off;
\item impose reality, $\delta(-\vk) = \delta(\vk)^*$, so that the field you get
back is real rather than complex;
\item inverse transform.
\end{enumerate}
Nothing else is needed. Two realizations drawn with different random seeds are
different universes with identical statistics, which is why a fixed seed is the
right way to compare cosmologies: change $n_s$ with the seed held, and every
difference you see is physics rather than luck.

\paragraph{Normalization: $\sigma_8$.} The spectrum has a shape and an
overall size, and the size is conventionally quoted as $\sigma_8$: the rms
fluctuation of the linear density field smoothed on spheres of radius
$8\,h^{-1}$Mpc,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_8^2 = \int\!\frac{\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\;\Plin(k)\,\big|W(kR)\big|^2 ,
\qquad R = 8\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc},
\label{eq:sigma8}
\end{equation}
with $W$ the Fourier transform of a top-hat sphere. The radius is historical
rather than fundamental --- it is roughly where $\sigma \simeq 1$, so it marks
the scale that is going nonlinear now --- but the definition is what normalizes
every spectrum you will plot. Measured, $\sigma_8 \simeq 0.81$.
\end{reading}

\begin{quiz}
Two seeds give completely different universes from the same $\Plin(k)$. In what
sense is $\Plin$ a prediction, then?
\nudge{From one universe, could you measure the amplitude at one particular
$\vk$, or only an average over modes of equal $|\vk|$? Which had better be the
thing theory predicts?}
\answer{The theory predicts the distribution, not the draw. The phases --- all
that differs between seeds --- are random and carry no cosmology; $\Plin$ is the
\emph{variance}, and that is the prediction. We measure it by averaging
$|\delta(\vk)|^2$ over modes of equal $|\vk|$, which are independent draws from
one distribution. The largest scales have the fewest such modes, so they are the
worst measured: cosmic variance is the price of owning one universe.}
\end{quiz}

\subsection{The primordial spectrum}
\label{sec:primordial}

Everything so far has been about how a perturbation \emph{evolves}. Where the
perturbations came from is the business of the lectures you have just had, and
we need only their output: a nearly \emph{scale-invariant} spectrum of curvature
perturbations $\mathcal{R}$, quoted as usual in dimensionless form,
\begin{equation}
\Delta^2_{\mathcal{R}}(k) \;\equiv\; \frac{k^3 P_{\mathcal{R}}(k)}{2\pi^2}
= A_s\left(\frac{k}{k_0}\right)^{n_s-1} ,
\qquad n_s \simeq 0.965 ,
\label{eq:primordial}
\end{equation}
with $A_s$ an amplitude and $k_0$ an arbitrary pivot scale. The exponent is
written $n_s-1$ so that $n_s = 1$ is exactly the scale-invariant case. This is
Dodelson \& Schmidt's Eq.~(7.99); much of the inflation literature writes $\zeta$
for the same quantity.

\begin{reading}
\bait{What is the pivot $k_0$, and does its value matter?}
It looks like physics and is not. A tilted power law has no single amplitude ---
$\Delta^2_{\mathcal{R}}$ takes a different value at every $k$ --- so $A_s$ has to
be quoted \emph{somewhere}, and $k_0$ is that somewhere: $A_s$ is by definition
$\Delta^2_{\mathcal{R}}(k_0)$. Move the pivot and $A_s$ moves with it, by
$(k_0'/k_0)^{n_s-1}$, leaving $\Delta^2_{\mathcal{R}}(k)$ unchanged at every $k$.
Planck's $A_s = 2.1\times10^{-9}$ at $k_0 = 0.05\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ would be
$2.35\times10^{-9}$ had they chosen $0.002\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$; the spectrum is the
same spectrum. The value is picked so that the data constrain $A_s$ and $n_s$
close to independently, which puts it near the middle of the measured range of
scales.

Two practical warnings. That $0.05$ is in ${\rm Mpc}^{-1}$, not
$h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$: in the units these lectures use it is
$0.074\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$, and reading it as $0.05\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ misplaces
the pivot by a factor $1/h$. And these lectures never need its value at all,
because we fix $A$ in \eqref{eq:pk_master} with $\sigma_8$ rather than with $A_s$, since the pivot cancels out of everything downstream.
\end{reading}

\begin{reading}
\bait{What \emph{is} $\mathcal{R}$, and how does it turn into $\delta$?}
$\mathcal{R}$ is a perturbation to the spatial curvature of a constant-time
slice, measured on slices where the matter is at rest: the spatial metric reads
$a^2(1+2\mathcal{R})\delta_{ij}$, so $\mathcal{R}$ is a local, dimensionless
shift in how much the universe has expanded at that point. Its virtue is that it
does not evolve outside the horizon, in any background, which is why inflation's
output is quoted in it: it can be handed forward unchanged, across the radiation
era and into the matter era, to the moment the mode enters and physics resumes.

Turning it into $\delta$ is two steps. First, deep in matter domination the
potential is a fixed fraction of it, $|\Phi| = \tfrac35|\mathcal{R}|$, a pure number with no $k$ in it. Second, Poisson converts the potential into a density,
and \emph{that} is where the two powers of $k$ come from. Together, for modes
well inside the horizon,
\begin{equation*}
\delta_m(k,a) = \frac{2}{5}\,\frac{k^2}{\Om H_0^2}\,\mathcal{R}(k)\,T(k)\,D_+(a) ,
\end{equation*}
which is Dodelson \& Schmidt's Eq.~(8.7): the $2/5$ is $\tfrac23\times\tfrac35$,
the $k^2$ is Poisson, $T$ handles modes that entered before equality, and $D_+$
carries the growth. Square it and the $k^4$ against $P_{\mathcal{R}} \propto
k^{n_s-4}$ gives $\Plin \propto k^{n_s}T^2D_+^2$ --- \eqref{eq:pk_master}, with
$A = 8\pi^2A_s k_0^{1-n_s}/25H_0^4\Om^2$.

One warning when you open the book: they write the spatial metric as
$a^2(1+2\Phi)$ where \eqref{eq:newtonian_gauge} writes $a^2(1-2\phi)$, so their
$\Phi$ is minus ours. The relation above is sign-consistent as written; the
intermediate potentials are not.
\end{reading}

\begin{reading}
\bait{Why is $n_s$ not exactly $1$?}
Scale invariance means equal power in every logarithmic interval of $k$, which is
what $n_s = 1$ would give. Inflation cannot deliver it exactly: inflation has to
end, so the expansion rate drifts while modes are leaving the horizon, and the
spectrum tilts. The measured few-percent deficit is a genuine prediction rather
than a fitted convenience, and it is one of the reasons to believe the picture.
\end{reading}

Two consequences carry forward. The primordial field is Gaussian to excellent
approximation, which is what Section~\ref{sec:stats} assumed. And
\eqref{eq:primordial} becomes a \emph{matter} spectrum by two steps that all but
cancel. Reading $P_{\mathcal{R}}$ off the definition divides by $k^3$; the Poisson
equation relating curvature to density then multiplies by $k^4$, since it costs
two powers of $k$ in the field and so four in the spectrum. Net one power of
$k$:
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm prim}(k) = A\,k^{n_s} ,
\label{eq:pprim}
\end{equation}
a featureless power law with no scale anywhere in it. Note where scale
invariance actually sits: $n_s = 1$ means $P_{\rm prim} \propto k$, not
$P_{\rm prim}$ constant.

\subsection{Horizon crossing, and the shape of \texorpdfstring{$\Plin(k)$}{P(k)}}
\label{sec:linearpk}

Write the linear spectrum as the primordial one times a \emph{transfer function}
that carries all the processing:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\Plin(k,z) \;=\; A\,k^{n_s}\,T^2(k)\,D_+^2(z)\;}
\label{eq:pk_master}
\end{equation}
with $T \to 1$ on the largest scales by convention and $A$ fixed by one measured
amplitude, for which we use $\sigma_8$. The three factors are three separate
pieces of physics: inflation sets $k^{n_s}$, the growth factor $D_+^2$ carries
the time dependence of Section~\ref{sec:vlasov}, and $T^2(k)$ carries everything
in between. This is the formula you will code in the hands-on session, and the
question now is what shapes $T$.

\begin{quiz}
The primordial spectrum is a featureless power law, yet the observed one has a
turnover, a falloff and wiggles. Where do those scales come from?
\nudge{None of them is primordial. One is set by the expansion: when did a mode come into causal contact? Another by the contents: when did matter overtake radiation? A third is a distance a wave could travel.}
\answer{All of them are imprinted after inflation, by two pieces of physics.
Horizon entry gives the turnover and the $k^{-2}$ falloff: modes entering during
radiation domination are suppressed, and the boundary is $k_{\rm eq}$, the mode
entering exactly at equality. The wiggles come from the sound horizon at
recombination, $r_d \simeq 100\,h^{-1}$Mpc, the farthest a pressure wave
travelled before the photons were released. Inflation supplies only an amplitude
and a tilt.}
\end{quiz}

One thing does: \textbf{when a mode entered the horizon}.

\paragraph{The horizon, and what crosses it.} Section~\ref{sec:bridge} defined
the comoving Hubble radius $1/\cH$ and used it to separate sub-horizon modes
from super-horizon ones. The point now is that the separation is not permanent:
$1/\cH$ changes with time while a comoving $k$ does not, so a given mode can
start on one side and end on the other. It does, and that is the whole
mechanism.

\begin{reading}
\bait{Have I been writing $H$ or $\cH$ --- and what was $1/\cH$ doing during
inflation?}
Not the Hubble parameter $H = \dd\ln a/\dd t$: overdots here are
$\partial/\partial\tau$ throughout, so $\dot a/a$ is $\cH$, not $H$, and it is
also why the growth equation \eqref{eq:growth} carries $\cH\dot D$ where its
cosmic-time form carries $2H\,\dd D/\dd t$.

You met this quantity in the previous lectures, running the other way. During
inflation $1/\cH$ \emph{shrinks}, which is how modes exit and how their
fluctuations are frozen in as the initial conditions of
Section~\ref{sec:primordial}. Afterwards it grows, through radiation and matter
domination alike, so a mode --- labelled by a comoving $\vk$ that never changes
--- starts super-horizon and \emph{enters} when $k = \cH$. Nothing about the mode
changes at that moment; the horizon moves past it.
\end{reading}

What follows is what that
crossing does, and Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon} is the picture to keep in mind while
it happens.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

$1/\cH$; the coloured lines are three modes, each at its fixed comoving $1/k$. A

$\cH(a_{\rm eq}) = 0.0153\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ for $\Om = 0.31$, $h = 0.676$ and

\newline
The curve bends because the figure spans three eras, and $1/\cH$ is a different

the plotted curve measures ($+0.998$ at $a = 10^{-6}$, $+0.514$ at $a =
10^{-2}$, $-0.534$ today and still steepening). The break at $a_{\rm eq}$ is
what makes $k_{\rm eq}$ special, and the slope of $+1$ to its left is exactly
the statement $a_{\rm enter}\propto 1/k$ that \eqref{eq:transfer_scaling} rests

Follow the potential $\Phi$ rather than the density. It is unambiguous, since
Section~\ref{sec:bridge} fixed the gauge completely, and it obeys three rules.

\emph{Outside the horizon, $\Phi$ holds still.} No causal process operates across
the mode, so nothing can reshape it. This does not follow from
\eqref{eq:newtonian_linear} --- those equations assumed $k \gg \cH$ --- and it is
not used to \emph{evolve} anything: it says there is nothing to evolve, so the
mode reaches entry still carrying the primordial amplitude of
Section~\ref{sec:primordial}. Super-horizon is where the initial condition sits,
not a phase we integrate through.

\emph{Inside the horizon during radiation domination, $\Phi$ decays.} Sub-horizon
the energy constraint is again a Poisson equation, $-k^2\Phi = 4\pi
Ga^2\bar\rho\,\delta$. The constraint cares only about what dominates the
density, here radiation. The \emph{dynamics} of \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear} do
not carry over: radiation has pressure, which is exactly what ``pressureless''
excluded, so how $\delta_r$ behaves is imported here rather than derived. Two
things then happen at once. The prefactor falls: $\bar\rho_r \propto a^{-4}$, so
$a^2\bar\rho_r \propto a^{-2}$. And the source refuses to grow: radiation
pressure resists compression, so $\delta_r$ does not collapse but rings, as a
sound wave in the tightly coupled photon--baryon plasma. Hence $\Phi \propto a^{-2}\times(\text{oscillation})$. Keep both factors in view. They become the two features of the spectrum: the
$a^{-2}$ envelope sets the broadband shape, and the oscillation is the BAO.

\emph{Inside the horizon during matter domination, $\Phi$ holds still again.}
Now $a^2\bar\rho_m \propto a^{-1}$ while $\delta$ grows as $D_+ \propto a$, and
the two cancel exactly. This is why the potential is constant through the whole
matter era, and why $\Phi$ stayed small in \eqref{eq:phi_small}.

Now assemble. A mode with $k < k_{\rm eq}$ enters the horizon \emph{after}
equality and so never sees the decaying phase: it arrives with its primordial
amplitude intact, $T \simeq 1$. Note what that does \emph{not} say. Such a mode
does enter --- $k = 10^{-3}\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ crosses in at $a \simeq 0.03$,
and has $T = 0.99$ --- but by then $\Phi$ has stopped decaying, so entry costs
it nothing. Suppression requires entering while radiation still dominates, not
entering as such. A mode with $k > k_{\rm eq}$ enters during
radiation domination, at $a_{\rm enter}$, and decays from then until equality.
Now just evaluate the decay between its two endpoints. Nothing happens before
$a_{\rm enter}$ and nothing happens after $a_{\rm eq}$, and in between
$\Phi \propto a^{-2}$, so the \emph{whole} accumulated suppression is that power
law read across the window,
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\Phi(a_{\rm eq})}{\Phi_{\rm prim}}
= \left(\frac{a_{\rm enter}}{a_{\rm eq}}\right)^{\!2} ,
\end{equation*}
and it is frozen in from then on. That is the factor, and it is nothing more
than $a^{-2}$ evaluated at the ends of the interval. The earlier a mode enters,
the wider the window and the smaller the survivor. To turn that into a statement
about $k$, use the entry condition $k = \cH(a_{\rm enter})$ with
$\cH \propto 1/a$ during radiation domination, so that
$a_{\rm enter} \propto 1/k$; fixing the normalization by the mode that enters
exactly at equality, $a_{\rm enter}/a_{\rm eq} = k_{\rm eq}/k$. Hence
\begin{equation}
T(k) \;\sim\; \left(\frac{a_{\rm enter}}{a_{\rm eq}}\right)^{2}
\;\propto\; \left(\frac{k_{\rm eq}}{k}\right)^{2} .
\label{eq:transfer_scaling}
\end{equation}


That is the shape, from horizon crossing and the Poisson equation alone: flat
below the break, falling as $k^{-2}$ above it, with the break at
$k_{\rm eq} \equiv \cH(a_{\rm eq}) \simeq 0.015\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$. It is not a
fitted parameter --- it depends on $\Om h^2$ and the radiation density --- and it
is the one scale the early universe stamps on the spectrum.

\begin{remark}
Two refinements, neither of which changes the argument. The cold dark matter is
not quite frozen while the potential decays --- it grows logarithmically, the
\emph{M\'esz\'aros effect} --- which softens the falloff to
$T \propto k^{-2}\ln k$. You can see this in a real transfer function: fitting
the Eisenstein \& Hu form over $0.5 < k < 5\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ gives a slope of
$-1.67$, not $-2$, and the difference is the logarithm. For the spectrum itself,
\eqref{eq:pk_master} therefore runs from $\Plin \propto k^{n_s}$ below the
turnover to $\Plin \propto k^{n_s-4}\ln^2 k$ far above it: a measured slope of
$-2.37$ over that same range, still well short of the asymptotic
$n_s - 4 = -3.04$, because the logarithm is in no hurry. And notice where that high-$k$ shape comes from: $k^{n_s-4}$ is the shape of $P_{\mathcal{R}}$
itself. The $k^2$ that Poisson puts into $\delta$ is exactly the $k^{-2}$ that
the decaying potential takes out, so a small-scale mode ends up with a
late-time $\delta$ proportional to its primordial $\mathcal{R}$ and no extra
scale dependence at all. Exercise~\ref{ex:meszaros}
works it out. And that oscillation survives as the baryon
acoustic wiggles: the plasma rings until recombination frees the photons, freezing
the wave at a single scale, the sound horizon $r_d \simeq 100\,h^{-1}$Mpc.
Baryons are only about a sixth of the matter, so the wiggles ride on the
broadband shape at the few-percent level rather than dominating it.
\end{remark}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:geodesic}
\textbf{From above.} Derive \eqref{eq:newtonian_eom}: write the geodesic equation for a nonrelativistic particle in the metric \eqref{eq:newtonian_gauge}, keep terms to first order in the potentials and to leading order in $u \ll 1$, and use $\phi = \psi = \Phi$. Careful: conformal time is not an affine parameter.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:eomnewton}
\textbf{From below.} Now reach \eqref{eq:newtonian_eom} with no relativity at
all. Start from momentum conservation in physical coordinates,
$\dd^2\vr/\dd t^2 = -\nabla_r\Phi_{\rm tot}$ with $\vr = a\vx$, and split
$\Phi_{\rm tot}$ into the part sourced by $\bar\rho$ and the part sourced by
$\bar\rho\,\delta$.
(i)~Using the Friedmann acceleration equation
$\ddot a = -\tfrac{4\pi}{3}G\bar\rho\,a$, show that the background gravity
cancels exactly, so that only the \emph{peculiar} potential survives. Why is
that cancellation not an accident of $p = 0$?
(ii)~Convert to conformal time and recover \eqref{eq:newtonian_eom}.
(iii)~Where did the drag term come from, and is it a force?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:subhorizon}
\textbf{The other limit.} Exercise~\ref{ex:geodesic} took $v\ll1$ and produced
the force law; this one takes $k\gg\cH$ and produces the field equation.
The linearized Einstein equation that becomes Poisson is the energy constraint
\cite[Eq.~(23a)]{MaBertschinger1995},
\begin{equation*}
\underbrace{k^2\phi}_{\text{survives}}
\;+\;\underbrace{3\cH\big(\dot\phi + \cH\psi\big)}_{\text{dropped}}
\;=\; -4\pi G a^2\bar\rho\,\delta .
\end{equation*}
(i) Reducing it to the Poisson equation of \eqref{eq:newtonian_linear} needs
slightly more than $k\gg\cH$: it needs $\dot\phi$ not to be anomalously large. Assuming
$\dot\phi \lesssim \cH\phi$, which holds for the growing mode, show that the
discarded group is smaller than the retained one by
$\mathcal{O}\big((\cH/k)^2\big)$.
(ii) Put a number on it. Today $\cH_0 = H_0/c \simeq 3.3\times10^{-4}\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$.
How large is the neglected term at $k = 0.1\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$, and at
$k = 10^{-3}\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$? Which of the two lies inside the window quoted
in the Comment above?
(iii) The assumption in (i) can be \emph{removed} rather than justified:
combining the time--time constraint with the time--space one gives an algebraic
equation for $\phi$ carrying no time derivative at all, in which the correction
is manifestly $\mathcal{O}(\cH/k)$ \cite[Ex.~6.7]{DodelsonSchmidt}. Why is
eliminating an assumption preferable to bounding it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:eds}
\textbf{Growth by hand.} In Einstein--de Sitter ($\Om = 1$), $a \propto \tau^2$
and $\cH = 2/\tau$, so \eqref{eq:growth} reads
$\ddot D + (2/\tau)\dot D - (6/\tau^2)D = 0$.
(i)~Try $D \propto \tau^n$ and factorize the indicial equation.
(ii)~Identify which root is the growing mode, and express both in terms of $a$
rather than $\tau$.
(iii)~A second-order equation has two solutions and the general one mixes them.
Relative to the growing mode, how fast does the other die, and what exactly does that erase?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:meszaros}
\textbf{The M\'esz\'aros effect.} Take a universe of cold dark matter and
radiation, with the radiation smooth --- it does not cluster --- but dominating
the expansion until equality. Using $y \equiv a/a_{\rm eq}$ as the time
variable, the CDM density contrast obeys
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd^2\delta}{\dd y^2}
+ \frac{2+3y}{2y(1+y)}\,\frac{\dd\delta}{\dd y}
- \frac{3}{2y(1+y)}\,\delta = 0 .
\end{equation*}
(i) Verify that $\delta \propto 1 + \tfrac32 y$ solves it.
(ii) Show that this is frozen for $y\ll1$ and grows as $a$ for $y\gg1$: a mode
sitting inside the horizon during radiation domination gains almost nothing
until equality.
(iii) Section~\ref{sec:linearpk} got the suppression of $T(k)$ from the
\emph{potential} decaying while a mode waits. Here it comes from the
\emph{density} refusing to grow. Are these two effects or one, and which is
easier to turn into a number?
\end{exercise}

\subsection{The Lagrangian picture}
\label{sec:lpt}

How does gravitational collapse actually proceed? A slightly overdense region
decelerates the matter around it; particles drift toward it, overshoot, and
eventually orbit. The natural variable is not the density at a fixed point but
the trajectory of each mass element. Label each element by its initial comoving
position $\vq$ and track its displacement,
\begin{equation}
\vx(\vq,\tau) = \vq + \vPsi(\vq,\tau),
\qquad \vPsi(\vq,0)=0 .
\label{eq:displacement}
\end{equation}
This is Lagrangian perturbation theory (LPT). The particle equation of motion \eqref{eq:newtonian_eom} governs $\vPsi$; taking its divergence and using the Poisson equation,
\begin{equation}
\nabla_x\cdot\left[\ddot\vPsi + \cH\,\dot\vPsi\right]
= -\,\frac{3}{2}\,\Om(\tau)\,\cH^2\,\delta\big(\vx(\vq,\tau)\big) .
\label{eq:lpt_master}
\end{equation}
The density needs no equation of its own. Mass conservation, $\bar\rho\,\dd^3q = \rho(\vx)\,\dd^3x$, fixes it algebraically from the volume distortion of the map:
\begin{equation}
1 + \delta(\vx,\tau) = \frac{1}{J(\vq,\tau)},
\qquad
J = \det\left(\delta_{ij} + \Psi_{i,j}\right),
\qquad
\Psi_{i,j} \equiv \frac{\partial\Psi_i}{\partial q_j} .
\label{eq:jacobian}
\end{equation}
A volume element that shrinks ($J<1$) is overdense; one that expands is
underdense, and Figure~\ref{fig:lagmap} is that sentence drawn. Because the
determinant is nonlinear in $\vPsi$, a displacement correct only at first order
already generates density perturbations at every order: low-order LPT resums an
infinite subset of Eulerian nonlinearities, the ones that come from moving
matter around. The subset is restricted, though: the Jacobian generates every power of the
linear displacement, not every dynamical nonlinearity, so more orders in
$\delta$ does not buy more dynamical accuracy.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{lagrangian_map.pdf}
\caption{The Lagrangian map and its Jacobian. \emph{Left:} a regular grid of
labels $\vq$, with an arrow to where the Zel'dovich displacement sends each one.
The labels are bookkeeping and never move; the matter does. \emph{Right:} the

three-dimensional spectrum on a two-dimensional field has no meaningful
normalization of its own.}
\label{fig:lagmap}
\end{figure}

At first order, \eqref{eq:lpt_master} with $\delta = -\nabla_q\cdot\vPsi^{(1)}$ (from \eqref{eq:jacobian}) says that the divergence of the displacement obeys the linear growth equation \eqref{eq:growth}. Since the flow is irrotational, $\vPsi^{(1)} = -\nabla_q\varphi^{(1)}$ for a Lagrangian potential $\varphi^{(1)}$, and matching to the Eulerian linear density gives
\begin{equation}
\nabla_q\cdot\vPsi^{(1)}(\vq,\tau) = -\,\delta^{(1)}(\vq,\tau)
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\vPsi^{(1)}(\vk,\tau) = \frac{i\vk}{k^2}\,\delta^{(1)}(\vk,\tau) .
\label{eq:za_displacement}
\end{equation}
\begin{quiz}
The density is $1+\delta = 1/J$, with no equation of its own. What happens when
$J \to 0$, and what is that physically?
\nudge{$J$ is the volume of a fluid element. What does zero volume mean for the
map $\vq \mapsto \vx$? Try one dimension, with two particles approaching.}
\answer{An element crushed to zero volume: $\delta$ diverges, and
$\vq \mapsto \vx$ stops being one-to-one because two labels have reached the
same place. This is \emph{shell crossing}, the first caustic --- a real feature
of collisionless collapse, not an artefact of the approximation --- and it is
where the fluid description fails. Section~\ref{sec:shellcross} picks it up.}
\end{quiz}

\subsection{The Zel'dovich approximation}
\label{sec:za}

The \emph{Zel'dovich approximation} (ZA) \cite{Zeldovich1970} takes this linear displacement seriously beyond linear order in the density: move particles on straight lines (in comoving coordinates) and read the density
off the Jacobian. Getting the density is three steps, and they are worth doing
slowly.

\emph{The deformation matrix is symmetric.} Since $\vPsi^{(1)} =
-\nabla_q\varphi^{(1)}$, its gradient is a matrix of second derivatives,
$\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j} = -\,\partial^2\varphi^{(1)}/\partial q_i\,\partial q_j$, and
mixed partials of a smooth function commute. So $\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j}$ is a Hessian,
hence symmetric. That is not an accident of first order: it follows from the
flow being irrotational, which is what gives the deformation matrix real
eigenvalues at all.

\emph{So it diagonalizes.} A real symmetric matrix has three real eigenvalues
and three orthogonal eigenvectors. At each $\vq$ separately there are therefore
three perpendicular axes along which the displacement does nothing but stretch
or compress. Write the eigenvalues as $-D_+\lambda_1$, $-D_+\lambda_2$,
$-D_+\lambda_3$: the growth factor pulls out because $\vPsi^{(1)} \propto D_+$,
which leaves the $\lambda_i$ fixed once and for all by the initial conditions,
and the minus sign is chosen so that positive $\lambda$ means collapse.

\emph{The determinant is then a product.} In that frame $\delta_{ij} +
\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j}$ is diagonal with entries $1-D_+\lambda_i$, and the determinant
of a diagonal matrix is the product of its entries. With $1+\delta = 1/J$ from
\eqref{eq:jacobian},
\begin{equation}
J = \prod_{i=1}^{3}\left(1 - D_+\lambda_i\right)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\delta_{\rm ZA}(\vx,\tau)
= \frac{1}{\left(1-D_+\lambda_1\right)\left(1-D_+\lambda_2\right)\left(1-D_+\lambda_3\right)} - 1 .
\label{eq:za_density}
\end{equation}
Each axis contributes one factor, and each factor can vanish on its own. That is
the whole of Section~\ref{sec:web}.

As a recipe this is completely explicit, which is why every initial-condition
generator uses it. Draw $\delta^{(1)}(\vk)$ with variance $\Plin(k)$, multiply
by $i\vk/k^2$ to get $\vPsi^{(1)}$, transform back, and move each particle from
$\vq$ to $\vq + \vPsi^{(1)}$. Three Fourier transforms and a shift, and you have
a nonlinear density field.

The velocities come free. Since $\vPsi^{(1)} \propto D_+$ and nothing else in it
depends on time,
\begin{equation}
\vu_{\rm ZA} = \dot{\vPsi}^{(1)} = \frac{\dot D_+}{D_+}\,\vPsi^{(1)}
= \cH f\,\vPsi^{(1)} ,
\label{eq:za_velocity}
\end{equation}
so positions and velocities are the same field up to the factor $\cH f$ of
\eqref{eq:growth_rate}. A simulation needs both, and the ZA supplies them together, one of the reasons it, and its second-order extension in
Section~\ref{sec:2lpt}, are what every code uses to start.

\begin{remark}
The ZA is not a degraded linear theory, and it is worth being clear why. Linear
theory says the \emph{density} is small. The ZA instead says the
\emph{displacement} is well approximated at first order, and then treats the consequences exactly, through a determinant that contains every power of
$\vPsi^{(1)}$. Since bulk flows are the dominant motion on quasi-linear scales,
that is the right thing to get right. It is why the ZA follows matter into
sheets, where linear theory only says $\delta$ is growing, and why it remains a
serviceable description well past the point where $\delta = 1$.
\end{remark}
\subsection{Why a web, and not a collection of spheres}
\label{sec:web}

You have already met a model of gravitational collapse: a uniform overdense
sphere, which contracts, turns around and collapses to a point. It is the
standard toy problem, it is analytically solvable, and it is the basis of most
of what is said about halo formation.

It also essentially never happens.

Look at what a sphere requires in \eqref{eq:za_density}: all three factors must
vanish together, $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \lambda_3$. But $\Psi^{(1)}_{i,j}$ is
the second-derivative matrix of a Gaussian random field --- a random symmetric
$3\times3$ matrix --- and degenerate eigenvalues among such matrices form a set
of measure zero. Sample four hundred thousand of them and you will not find one.
\emph{Exactly spherical collapse has probability zero.}

So the generic situation is $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 > \lambda_3$, strictly, and
the three factors in \eqref{eq:za_density} vanish at three different times. That
single fact is the origin of the cosmic web. Collapse runs along the $\lambda_1$
axis first and the patch flattens into a \emph{sheet}, a Zel'dovich pancake. If
$\lambda_2 > 0$ the sheet later contracts along a second axis into a
\emph{filament}. If $\lambda_3 > 0$ as well, the filament finally draws up into a
\emph{knot}. How far a patch gets is decided by nothing more than how many of its eigenvalues are positive, and that is fixed in the initial conditions, before any
dynamics has happened.

That makes the morphology countable. For a Gaussian field the joint distribution
of the three eigenvalues is known \cite{Doroshkevich1970}, and the volume
fractions come out as
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcl@{}}
\toprule
positive eigenvalues & fraction of volume & fate \\
\midrule
0 & $8\%$  & void, expanding in all three directions \\
1 & $42\%$ & sheet \\
2 & $42\%$ & filament \\
3 & $8\%$  & knot \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{quiz}
What does a sphere require of $\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3$, and how often
does a random symmetric matrix oblige? Then, before you look at the numbers:
which two of void, sheet, filament and knot should be commonest, and why is the
table symmetric?
\nudge{To stay spherical, how must the eigenvalues be related? Write down any
$3\times3$ symmetric matrix. Were two of its eigenvalues equal? For the
second half, treat each eigenvalue's sign as a coin flip and count.}
\answer{A sphere needs $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \lambda_3$, a probability-zero
coincidence: spherical collapse describes an event that never happens. Generic
collapse is anisotropic, running fastest along the largest eigenvalue: sheet, then filament, then knot. Three fair coins would give $12.5/37.5/37.5/12.5$ per
cent; the true $8/42/42/8$ keeps the symmetry, since a Gaussian field prefers no
sign of $\delta$, but shifts weight to the mixed cases, because the eigenvalues
repel rather than being independent.}
\end{quiz}

Read the middle rows: \textbf{$84\%$ of the volume is headed for a sheet or a
filament, and only $8\%$ ever collapses along all three axes.} The web is not
decoration draped over a population of spherical halos. It is what almost
everything becomes, and the compact objects are the rare case.

This is also why the ZA is worth more than its accuracy suggests. It predicts the
skeleton of the cosmic web --- where the sheets and filaments will be --- from
the second derivatives of the initial potential alone, with no dynamics
whatsoever. The hands-on session that follows this lecture puts that to the test:
you will displace a Gaussian field once, classify each particle by how many of its
eigenvalues are collapsing, and get a web out.

\subsection{Shell crossing, and where the approximation fails}
\label{sec:shellcross}

The first of those collapses is also the end of the theory. When
$D_+\lambda_1 \to 1$ the first factor in \eqref{eq:za_density} vanishes, the
density formally diverges, and $J \to 0$. That moment is \emph{shell crossing}:
particle trajectories intersect, the map $\vq \mapsto \vx$ stops being
invertible, and several streams with different velocities occupy the same
point. The fluid description of Section~\ref{sec:bridge} assumed exactly one
velocity at each position --- that is what cold meant --- so it ends here, and
the Zel'dovich solution ends with it.

Be clear, then, about what the later stages of Fig.~\ref{fig:collapse} are.
Formally the second factor vanishes later wherever $\lambda_2>0$ and the third
later still wherever $\lambda_3>0$, and the sequence sheet $\rightarrow$ filament
$\rightarrow$ knot is that ordering of eigenvalue crossings. As a guide to
\emph{morphology} it is excellent, and it is what the table above counts. As
\emph{dynamics} past the first caustic it is an extrapolation: a Zel'dovich knot
is not a halo, and the approximation says nothing about binding, virialization,
or the stable orbiting configuration that collapsed matter actually settles into.
Getting through shell crossing needs adhesion models, phase-space methods, or
$N$-body simulation, which is precisely why the initial conditions of
Section~\ref{sec:2lpt} matter, since a simulation is what you hand them to.

\subsection{Second order: 2LPT and initial conditions}
\label{sec:2lpt}

At second order the displacement responds to the gravitational tide generated at first order. Expanding \eqref{eq:lpt_master} and \eqref{eq:jacobian} to second order, the divergence $\Psi^{(2)}_{i,i}$ obeys the linear-growth operator with a quadratic source \cite[Eqs.~(E.30)--(E.31)]{Jeong2010}; separating variables, the second-order growth factor obeys
\begin{equation}
\ddot D_2 + \cH\dot D_2 - \frac{3}{2}\Om\cH^2 D_2
= -\frac{3}{2}\Om\cH^2 D_+^2 ,
\qquad
D_2(\tau) \simeq -\frac{3}{7}\,D_+^2(\tau)\;\Om^{-1/143},
\label{eq:D2}
\end{equation}
where the fit is better than $0.1\%$ over $10^{-3}<a<1$ for flat \lcdm{} and the
EdS limit is $D_2 = -\tfrac37 D_+^2$. (This Lagrangian $D_2$ is a different object from the second-order amplitude of Eulerian perturbation theory \cite{EFTnotes}; the literature uses the same symbol for both.) The spatial source is the second principal invariant of the deformation tensor $\varphi^{(1)}_{,ij}$: writing $\vPsi^{(2)} = \nabla_q\varphi^{(2)}$,
\begin{equation}
\nabla_q^2\varphi^{(2)}
\simeq -\frac{3}{7}\,\Om^{-1/143}
\sum_{i>j}\Big[\varphi^{(1)}_{,ii}\,\varphi^{(1)}_{,jj} - \big(\varphi^{(1)}_{,ij}\big)^2\Big],
\label{eq:2lpt_potential}
\end{equation}
with $\vPsi^{(1)} = -\nabla_q\varphi^{(1)}$ and $\nabla^2_q\varphi^{(1)} = \delta^{(1)}$, both understood as full-time fields with their growth factors included. Do not read \eqref{eq:2lpt_potential} as a purely tidal source: the invariant decomposes into an isotropic piece built from $\delta^2$ and a trace-free tidal piece, and it is nonzero even for a spherical patch, as Exercise~\ref{ex:d2} makes explicit. The full 2LPT map, including velocities, is
\begin{equation}
\vx = \vq - \nabla_q\varphi^{(1)} + \nabla_q\varphi^{(2)},
\qquad
\vu = -\cH f_1 \nabla_q\varphi^{(1)} + \cH f_2\nabla_q\varphi^{(2)},
\qquad
f_2 \simeq 2\,\Om^{6/11} ,
\label{eq:2lpt_map}
\end{equation}
where $f_1 \equiv \dd\ln D_+/\dd\ln a = f$ and, because the convention here has $D_2<0$ so that $\ln D_2$ is undefined,
\begin{equation}
f_2 \equiv \frac{\dd\ln|D_2|}{\dd\ln a} = \frac{\dot D_2}{\cH D_2} .
\label{eq:f2_def}
\end{equation}
The velocity in \eqref{eq:2lpt_map} is unaffected by this: the sign of $D_2$ already sits in $\varphi^{(2)}$. Physically: Zel'dovich streams are straight; the tide bends them. 2LPT is the standard for setting up initial conditions of $N$-body simulations: starting from the ZA alone leaves slowly decaying transients, and 2LPT strongly suppresses them. How large the residual is depends on the starting redshift, the force resolution, and which statistic you look at \cite[App.~E.5]{Jeong2010}.

\begin{remark}
Sign conventions differ across the literature, so it is worth saying which one
this is. Everything in this lecture --- the displacement $\vx = \vq + \vPsi$, the
Jacobian, the deformation tensor and its eigenvalues, the growth equation for
$D_2$ and its fit, the second-order source, and the map \eqref{eq:2lpt_map}
itself --- follows Jeong \cite[App.~E]{Jeong2010} exactly, including $D_2 < 0$
with $+\nabla_q\varphi^{(2)}$ in the map. Formulae carry across in either
direction without adjustment. Other references absorb the minus sign into $D_2$
instead; check before comparing codes.
\end{remark}

Do the Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions carry the same information?
Order by order, and before shell crossing, yes: the companion notes
\cite{EFTnotes} make the correspondence explicit by recovering the Eulerian
kernels from the displacement map.

\subsection{Galaxies: linear bias}
\label{sec:bias}

Everything so far has been about matter. Surveys measure galaxies, and galaxies
are not matter.

They live in \emph{halos} --- collapsed, virialized lumps of dark matter, the
objects Section~\ref{sec:shellcross} said the Zel'dovich approximation cannot
make --- and halos sample the density field unfairly. On large scales,
\begin{equation}
\delta_g(\vx) = b_1\,\delta(\vx) ,
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
P_g(k) = b_1^2\,\Plin(k) ,
\label{eq:linear_bias}
\end{equation}
\begin{quiz}
Why should $b_1$ exceed one? Galaxies are made of matter, so why do they not simply trace it?
\nudge{Galaxies form above a density threshold. Add a long mode that lifts the
background slightly: the density barely moved, but how much did the \emph{number
of sites above threshold} move? Sketch a Gaussian with a cut in its tail and
slide it right.}
\answer{Thresholds amplify: galaxies form only above a cut sitting in the tail,
so a small shift of the whole field carries a lot of that tail across it. The
rest of this section turns that sentence into a number.}
\end{quiz}

with a single number $b_1$, the \emph{linear bias}. Rather than assert
$b_1 > 1$, we can derive it, in a model crude enough to do on the board and
sharp enough to get the mechanism right \cite[\S 2.1]{BiasReview}.

\paragraph{Halos as regions above a threshold.} Smooth the same field
$\delta^{(1)}(\vq)$ this lecture has been displacing on a scale $R$, and declare
a halo wherever it exceeds a fixed $\delta_{\rm cr}$. That is the entire model:
no dynamics, one number, the same initial field as Section~\ref{sec:web} sorted
by a different criterion. The number is $\delta_{\rm cr} \simeq 1.686$, the
linearly extrapolated density at which a uniform sphere would collapse to a
point.

\begin{reading}
\bait{Section~\ref{sec:web} said spherical collapse never happens. So why is a
threshold taken from spherical collapse allowed here?}
A fair objection, and the two statements are about different things.
Section~\ref{sec:web} is about \emph{morphology}: exact degeneracy of the three
eigenvalues has probability zero, so nothing collapses as a sphere and the web
is not a population of balls. Here $\delta_{\rm cr}$ is used only as a
\emph{number}, a rough scale for how deep a well must be before it holds
together. Nothing in the derivation of \eqref{eq:pbs} requires the collapse to
be spherical. A different threshold shifts $\nu_c$ and the value of $b_1$ and
changes nothing about the mechanism, which is one reason this model predicts a
mechanism rather than a number.
\end{reading} Since $\delta^{(1)}$ is Gaussian with variance
$\sigma^2(R)$, the fraction of space above the cut is
\begin{equation}
\bar n = \tfrac12\,\erfc\!\left(\frac{\nu_c}{\sqrt2}\right) ,
\qquad
\nu_c \equiv \frac{\delta_{\rm cr}}{\sigma(R)} ,
\label{eq:halo_abundance}
\end{equation}
so massive halos --- large $R$, hence small $\sigma(R)$, hence large $\nu_c$ ---
are exponentially rare. And $\sigma(R)$ is nothing new: $\sigma_8$ is this same
quantity at $R = 8\,h^{-1}$Mpc.

\paragraph{Why the count is biased.} Add a long mode $\delta_L$, constant across
a patch many halos wide. Lifting the field is the same as lowering the cut, to
$\delta_{\rm cr} - \delta_L$, so differentiate the abundance:
\begin{equation}
b_1^{\rm L} \;=\; \frac{1}{\bar n}\,\frac{\partial n}{\partial\delta_L}
\;=\; \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,
\frac{e^{-\nu_c^2/2}}{\sigma(R)\,\erfc\!\left(\nu_c/\sqrt2\right)}
\;\;\xrightarrow[\ \nu_c \gg 1\ ]{}\;\;
\frac{\delta_{\rm cr}}{\sigma^2(R)} ,
\label{eq:pbs}
\end{equation}
and there is the answer. Rarer objects sit further out in the tail and respond
harder, which is why the most massive galaxies are the most strongly biased, and why $b_1$ belongs to the tracer, not to the cosmology.
Figure~\ref{fig:threshold} is \eqref{eq:pbs} measured rather than illustrated.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{threshold_bias.pdf}
\caption{Bias, measured from the same linear field this lecture displaces.
\emph{Left:} a cut through $\delta^{(1)}$ smoothed on $R = 5\,h^{-1}$Mpc, where
$\sigma(R) = 0.665$ and so $\nu_c = 2.54$. Adding a long mode of amplitude
$0.3$ --- barely visible against the small-scale field --- takes this cut from
$5$ regions above threshold to $18$, and note where the new ones appear:
clustered where $\delta_L > 0$. The cut was chosen to contain a few peaks,
because most contain none; at $\bar n \simeq 6\times10^{-3}$ these objects are
genuinely rare. \emph{Right:} the count against the long mode. Points are
counted from the field, the curve is \eqref{eq:halo_abundance} with the
threshold shifted, and the dotted line is its slope at the origin,
\eqref{eq:pbs}. Measuring that slope from the counts returns $4.31$ against a
predicted $4.29$. Two cautions the picture makes plain: the curve is convex, so
a slope fitted across a wide range overestimates $b_1$ --- $5.1$ here --- and
the asymptotic form $\delta_{\rm cr}/\sigma^2 = 3.81$ is still $11\%$ low at
this rarity.}
\label{fig:threshold}
\end{figure}

Two things are worth knowing about \eqref{eq:linear_bias}. It is the first term
of an expansion, not a law, and the threshold model is far too crude to
predict a real $b_1$, which is measured alongside the cosmology. At smaller
scales the response stops being linear,
picks up a dependence on the tidal field, and acquires a stochastic piece. And it
is written in real space, whereas a survey infers radial positions from
redshifts, so peculiar velocities distort what is measured along the line of
sight. Both corrections, and the one-loop machinery that organises them, are the
subject of the companion notes \cite{EFTnotes}.

\begin{remark}
That closes the loop the course opened. Section~\ref{sec:linearpk} showed how to
get $\Plin(k)$ from a cosmological model, and it has since been put to two
distinct uses. Fed through the displacement map of this lecture it becomes
initial conditions for an $N$-body simulation, which is what the hands-on session
does. Multiplied by $b_1^2$ it becomes a prediction for what a galaxy survey
measures. One spectrum, two roads: the first leads to simulations of structure
formation, the second to cosmological inference from galaxy clustering.
\end{remark}
